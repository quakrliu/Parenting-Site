**BloomPath** gets asked this one constantly, usually right after a specific incident: "My kid used to be fine around dogs, and now she loses it the second she sees one — is that normal?" Yes. Fear of dogs is one of the most common animal fears in early childhood, and it tends to peak between ages 2 and 6. It's not a character flaw or a sign you've done something wrong. Two Saturdays ago, at the dog park near our house, our neighbor Rachel's golden retriever Biscuit trotted over to say hello the way goldens do — tail going, tongue out, completely harmless. My daughter grabbed my leg so hard I nearly lost my balance, buried her face in my jeans, and wouldn't look up until Biscuit's owner called him back. She'd played near that same dog three weeks earlier without blinking.

I texted Rachel an apology. Rachel, who has two kids of her own, texted back: "Oh, that's just Tuesday around here. It'll pass." I wasn't so sure. My daughter brought up dogs unprompted at least four times that week, and none of it was positive — a comment about a barking dog two houses down, a refusal to walk past our mail carrier's leashed terrier, a sudden interest in whether our own front door "locks all the way."

I want to be upfront about something: I don't have a tidy explanation for why the fear showed up exactly when it did. What I do have is what we actually tried, what the research says about why this happens, and where we landed a few months later.

## Is It Normal for a Toddler to Suddenly Be Afraid of Dogs?

Yes, and it's common enough that it has a name — animal phobias, with dogs at the top of the list, are among the most frequently reported fears in early childhood. Estimates on how many kids develop a clinical-level phobia vary by study, but a meaningful share of children show a strong fear reaction to dogs at some point before age 12, and the onset tends to cluster in the preschool and early elementary years.

Part of this is just cognitive timing. A toddler's imagination is developing faster than her ability to reason through risk. She can extrapolate from one bad or startling moment — a dog barking loudly, a dog jumping up, even a dog just moving unpredictably fast — into "dogs are dangerous," full stop. That's not irrational. It's actually a reasonably efficient way for a developing brain to generalize from limited data, even when the generalization overshoots.

## Why Did This Start Now, When She Was Fine Before?

Something usually shifts the frame, even if it looks minor to an adult. In our case, I eventually connected it to a moment at a birthday party where a dog barked sharply right next to her, and she hadn't seen it coming. She never mentioned it. I only put it together weeks later, comparing notes with my husband about what had happened that particular weekend.

Kids this age don't reliably self-report the triggering moment, so you may never get a clean answer. What matters more than the origin story is what reinforces the fear afterward — every time she avoids a dog and the anxious feeling drops, her brain logs that avoidance as the thing that worked. That's how a one-time startle turns into a standing rule.

## Does My Own Reaction Actually Change How Scared She Gets?

Yes, more than what you say to her. Developmental psychologist Joseph Campos and his colleagues ran a well-known study in the 1980s using the "visual cliff" — a table with a glass surface that creates the illusion of a steep drop. When babies reached the edge, they looked to their mother's face before deciding whether to cross. If she showed fear, almost none of the babies crossed. If she showed joy or interest, most of them did. This is called social referencing, and it doesn't stop being true once your kid turns three or four.

I noticed this myself once I started paying attention. If I tensed up even slightly when a dog approached — and I did, more than once, because Biscuit's enthusiasm genuinely startled me too — my daughter's grip on my leg got tighter within seconds. My calm, even when I had to fake some of it, changed her body language almost immediately. That's not a magic fix. It's one input among several. But it's the one I had the most control over.

## What Should I Actually Do in the Moment When She Freezes?

Don't force contact, and don't over-narrate the fear either. I found a technique developed by dog-safety educators — commonly taught to school-age kids as "be a tree" — that works surprisingly well for a scared toddler too: stop moving, fold your arms in, look down, and stay still and boring until the dog loses interest. I taught it to my daughter as a game before we ever needed it in a real moment, practicing with our own dog-loving cousin standing in for a strange dog. Having something specific to do, instead of just "calm down," gave her a script to follow instead of a blank space to panic in.

What I stopped doing: picking her up automatically the second a dog appeared. It felt kind in the moment, and sometimes it's genuinely necessary, but doing it reflexively taught her that the correct response to any dog was "get removed from the situation," which reinforced the avoidance loop instead of building a tolerance for staying present through discomfort.

## Should I Make Her Pet the Dog So She Gets Used to It?

No. This is the piece of advice I hear most often from well-meaning relatives, and it's the one I'd push back on hardest. Janet Lansbury, whose respectful parenting work has shaped a lot of how I think about consent with young kids, argues against forcing physical affection on children in general — hugs, kisses, greetings — because it teaches them their bodily boundaries are negotiable when an adult decides they should be. The same principle applies here. Forcing a scared child to touch a dog doesn't build confidence; it teaches her that her fear doesn't count as a reason to say no, which tends to make kids more anxious in these situations over time, not less.

Grandparents in particular sometimes push this one hard — "just pet it, it won't bite, don't be silly." I understand the instinct. It comes from wanting to fix the fear quickly. But a child who's told her fear doesn't matter learns to hide it rather than manage it, and hidden fear doesn't actually shrink.

## What Does Montessori Say About This?

Montessori classrooms treat courtesy and confidence around living things — plants, classroom pets, other people — as a skill you build through repeated, low-stakes practice, not something you either have or don't. My daughter's Montessori teacher once described it to me as "meeting the child where the fear actually is, not where you wish it were." In practice, that meant letting kids observe a classroom guinea pig from across the room for as long as they needed before anyone offered them the option to get closer, with zero pressure either way.

That reframing helped me more than almost anything else. I stopped measuring progress by whether she'd pet a dog and started measuring it by whether she could stand three feet away without crying, then two feet, then close enough to watch its tail wag without needing to grab my leg. Each of those was real progress, even though none of them looked like "cured."

## How Do You Actually Build the Confidence Without Forcing It?

Small, repeatable steps she controls the pace of, ideally with a calm, familiar dog rather than a strange one:

1. **Watch from a distance first.** We started by watching Biscuit play fetch from across the park — no interaction required, just proximity with an easy exit available.
2. **Let her choose the distance.** I stopped deciding how close was "close enough" and started asking her where she wanted to stand. Some days that was ten feet away. That still counted.
3. **Give her a job that doesn't require touching.** She liked tossing a treat toward Biscuit from a few feet away — contact without contact, and something she initiated instead of something done to her.
4. **Talk about dog behavior in plain language.** We talked through what a wagging tail means, what a growl means, and how to tell a relaxed dog from an alert one. Concrete facts gave her something to hold onto besides a general sense of danger.
5. **Keep sessions short and end on a good note.** Five calm minutes that end while she's still doing okay beats twenty minutes that end in a meltdown, every time.

## When Should I Actually Worry About This?

Most kids' fear of dogs fades or becomes manageable within a few months of low-pressure practice. It's worth a closer look — a conversation with your pediatrician or a child psychologist — if the fear is getting worse rather than better after a couple of months, if it's expanding to unrelated things (all animals, all loud noises, all strangers), or if it's limiting her daily life in ways that go beyond avoiding actual dogs, like refusing to go outside at all. That's a different situation than a kid working through a normal developmental fear, and it deserves different support.

It's also worth saying plainly: some caution around dogs isn't something to fully eliminate. Children ages 5 to 9 are bitten by dogs more than any other age group, and kids make up roughly half of the estimated 4.5 million dog bites that happen in the US every year, according to data compiled from CDC injury statistics. The goal was never to make my daughter fearless around every dog she meets. It was to help her tell the difference between healthy caution and a fear that was running her whole day.

## Where We Are Now

She still won't approach a dog she doesn't know without checking in with me first, which I'm fine with — that's not fear, that's a reasonable rule. But she can stand near Biscuit now without grabbing anyone's leg, she asked to toss him a treat unprompted last week, and the comments about our front door locking have stopped. It didn't resolve in one conversation or one exposure. It took a few months of small, boring, repeated practice, mostly on her terms.

I still catch myself tensing up sometimes when a big dog runs toward us. I'm working on that too.

## FAQ

**Is it normal for a toddler who used to be fine around dogs to suddenly become afraid of them?**
Yes. New fears, including fear of dogs, commonly emerge or intensify between ages 2 and 6 as imagination outpaces a child's ability to reason through risk. It doesn't always trace back to one clear incident, and it's not a sign of a parenting mistake.

**Should I force my child to pet a dog to help her get over the fear?**
No. Forcing contact tends to teach a child that her fear doesn't count as a reason to say no, which can make anxiety worse over time rather than better. A graded, child-paced approach — letting her choose the distance and the pace — works better than forced exposure.

**Does my own reaction to dogs affect my child's fear?**
Yes, significantly. Research on social referencing shows young children look to a caregiver's face and body language to decide whether something new is safe, often more than they respond to verbal reassurance. Staying visibly calm, even when you have to work at it, changes a child's response in the moment.

**What is the "be a tree" technique?**
It's a dog-safety technique originally developed to teach school-age kids what to do around an unfamiliar or overexcited dog: stop moving, fold your arms in, look down at your feet, and stay still and quiet until the dog loses interest. It also works well for a scared toddler because it gives them a concrete action instead of just "calm down."

**When should I be concerned about my child's fear of dogs?**
If the fear is getting worse rather than better after a couple of months of low-pressure practice, spreading to unrelated things, or significantly limiting daily activities beyond avoiding dogs themselves, it's worth talking to your pediatrician or a child psychologist.

**Is some fear of dogs actually reasonable for a child to have?**
Yes. Children ages 5 to 9 are bitten by dogs more than any other age group, so healthy caution around unfamiliar dogs isn't something to eliminate entirely. The goal is helping your child tell the difference between reasonable caution and fear that's running her whole day.
