It's Saturday, a little past noon. Your fourth-grader is flat on the couch with a tablet and says, without looking up, "Can you make me a sandwich?" Ten minutes later it's "Where are my shin guards?" Then "Can you cut me an apple?" You can feel something tightening in your chest, because you know this child can do all of it. Last month they built an entire Lego city by themselves. Yet somehow a sandwich is out of reach.

If that scene is familiar, this one is for you. BloomPath put it together for parents of 9 to 12 year olds: why capable kids stop doing things they can do, what to say in the moment, and how to rebuild everyday independence without turning the house into a battlefield.

## What should you do right now when your tween asks you to do something they can do?

Say yes to the connection and no to the task. Something like: "I'd love to sit with you while you make it. Want me to hand you the bread?" You stay close, you keep your voice friendly, and your hands stay in your pockets. The sandwich might be crooked. That's fine.

If your child pushes back with "You're so mean," skip the debate. "Maybe so. The bread's on the counter." Then walk away and do something else in the same room. The first few times, the whining gets louder before it fades. That's normal. Kids test whether the new rule is real.

Save the bigger conversation for a calm moment, not for the middle of a hungry Saturday. More on that below.

## Why does a capable 10-year-old stop doing things for themselves?

Because somewhere along the way, doing less got rewarded. Four common paths lead here:

- **It was faster.** At six, tying shoes took four minutes and you had a bus to catch. You tied them. Nobody noticed when "just this once" became the system.
- **Screens fill the gap.** When the tablet is more interesting than the kitchen, anything that interrupts the tablet feels like a cost. Asking you is cheaper than getting up.
- **Homework took over.** As school load grows, many families quietly drop household jobs so there's "more time to study." The child learns that real life is somebody else's department.
- **They're afraid of doing it wrong.** A perfectionist tween would rather not try than make a crooked sandwich and hear a comment about it. If this sounds like your child, the approach in [the perfectionist kid before a test](/en/blog/exam-night-perfectionist-tween-teen-en) applies here too.

Spotting which one is driving it changes your response. Speed habits need patience from you. Screen habits need a calmer transition. Fear needs reassurance that mistakes in the kitchen are cheap.

## What does Montessori say about independence at this age?

Maria Montessori's famous line for young children, "Help me to do it by myself," doesn't expire at six. She believed older children need real work too, work with a visible result that matters to other people. For adolescents she described a working-farm program she called Erdkinder, where teenagers ran things that mattered rather than rehearsing for adult life in a classroom.

Few of us have a farm, but the principle moves into a regular house easily. In Montessori terms you're building a **prepared environment** and then stepping back:

- The step stool, the knife, and the cutting board live where your child can reach them.
- The cereal bowls sit on a low shelf, not behind the glass cabinet doors.
- The laundry basket has a spot your child can reach, and a reminder they chose.
- A short list on the fridge says what "my jobs" are, written in your child's handwriting.

Montessori programs for older children treat setting up snack, washing dishes, and running small errands as ordinary parts of the day, with no applause attached. The work is just the work. That tone is what you're after at home: calm, expected, and unglamorous.

## How do you rebuild skills without a power struggle?

Start with a short conversation when everyone is fed and calm. Then follow these steps:

1. **Name what you've noticed, without blame.** "I've been doing a lot of the small stuff for you, and I think that stopped helping you. That's on me."
2. **Let your child pick.** Offer three jobs: make weekend lunch, own the laundry from hamper to drawer, or handle packing their own bag. Choice turns "you're being told" into "I'm running something."
3. **Teach it once, properly.** Show the full job, then do it together, then watch them do it, then step out. It's the same four-step sequence for a knife skill or a washing machine.
4. **Agree on "good enough" in advance.** If the standard is "your mother's folding," your child will quit. "Clean clothes in the drawer" is the standard.
5. **Pull your help out gradually.** Hand-over-hand this week, standing nearby next week, in another room the week after.
6. **Expect a dip.** Week two is usually the worst. The novelty has worn off and the new routine hasn't turned into a habit yet. Hold the line gently.

Jessica Lahey's *The Gift of Failure* makes the same case from a teacher's perspective: when parents keep rescuing kids from small struggles, children lose the chance to build competence and a sense that they can handle things.

## How should you talk about it so they hear it?

Pick language that gives your child some dignity. A few swaps that work:

- Instead of "You're so lazy," try "That's a lot of sitting. I'm guessing you're tired. What would make this job easier to start?"
- Instead of "I'm not your maid," try "This part of the house is a team job. Which part do you want?"
- Instead of redoing their work in front of them, leave it, or fix one thing later when they're not watching. Quietly redoing a job tells them their effort doesn't count.
- Instead of "Good job!" try "The towels are folded the way you said you'd do them. That saved me ten minutes."

Positive parenting makes a distinction here that I find helpful. You can be firmly kind: the expectation stays, and so does your warmth. Dr. Becky Kennedy's idea that your child is good inside and having a hard time fits well. A kid who avoids cooking isn't a bad kid. They've just never been asked to carry it. ## Should chores be paid?

Everyday family jobs, like dishes, laundry, and putting your own things away, tend to work better as contributions to the household than as paid work. Extra jobs outside the routine can earn money. We walk through the Montessori reasoning in [Should Kids Get Paid for Chores?](/en/blog/should-kids-be-paid-for-chores-montessori-en).

## Does it matter whether kids do household tasks?

It likely helps. A 2002 analysis by Marty Rossmann at the University of Minnesota, using data from a longitudinal study of 84 children, found that people who started doing household tasks at ages 3 to 4 tended to do better as young adults on measures of relationships, school, and early work than those who started as teenagers. It's a small, correlational study, so it can't prove chores cause success. What it does support is the idea that responsibility early in life belongs to a healthy pattern, and that it's never too late to start building it.

## When should you look closer?

Reluctance to do tasks is usually habit. Look closer if you see any of the following:

- Your child seems unable to do tasks they used to do easily, or seems slower and flatter than usual.
- They're avoiding school or friends along with chores.
- Sleep, appetite, or mood has changed for several weeks.
- Tasks that involve sequencing, memory, or fine motor control are hard even with a lot of coaching.

In those cases, talk to your pediatrician or your child's teacher. Attention differences, anxiety, and low mood can all look like "won't," when the real situation is "can't right now."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal for a 10-year-old to still ask parents to do small things for them?**
Yes. Many children this age lean on parents out of habit or convenience, especially when screens are nearby. The goal is to shift the habit gradually, not to judge it.

**What life skills can a 9 to 12 year old realistically do?**
Make a simple meal like eggs, pasta, or a sandwich, run a load of laundry, pack their own school bag, keep their room in reasonable order, and handle small errands like paying at a corner store. Start with one and add more over weeks.

**My child says they're too busy with homework. What now?**
Keep jobs short, around ten to fifteen minutes. A few minutes of real work is also a reset between study blocks, and most kids come back to homework more settled.

**What if my child does a job badly on purpose so I'll take over?**
Stay calm and describe the result: "The floor still has crumbs. Do you want to finish, or shall we do it together?" Don't take over. That response keeps the job theirs.

**Does this approach work for kids who find it hard to focus?**
Often it does, with smaller steps, a visual checklist, and more coaching. If tasks are consistently hard despite support, ask your pediatrician about attention and executive function.

**How long until it becomes routine?**
Most families notice a real shift in four to eight weeks of steady expectations. Expect ups and downs.
