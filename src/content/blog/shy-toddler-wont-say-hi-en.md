**TL;DR:** It's a familiar moment — a child buries their face in a parent's leg instead of saying hi, and someone in the room calls it rude. It usually isn't rudeness. It's temperament, and forcing the greeting almost always makes it worse. Model the greeting yourself, narrate for your child instead of prompting them, give them a warm-up window before any interaction, and save the "manners talk" for a calm moment at home — not in front of the audience that triggered it.

If you've been to a family gathering with a young child, you may know this moment. A relative bends down, arms half-open, and says, "Hi! You remember me, right?" Your child turns, grabs two fistfuls of your jeans, and disappears behind your leg like you're a piece of furniture they could climb inside.

Someone laughs it off — "oh, she's shy" — but someone else doesn't let it go so easily. "Honey, say hi. It's not hard." A little sing-song, a little pointed. Your child presses their face harder into your leg. And you're standing there doing the math: do you make them do it and end this awkward moment, or let it drag on and look like you're raising a kid with no manners?

A lot of us do the same thing the first few times. We crouch down and say, "Say hi to Auntie," in our brightest voice. The child shakes their head, buries deeper. Now there are three adults staring at a three-year-old, waiting.

Nothing about that moment makes a child more likely to say hi. If anything, it can mean more hiding at the next gathering, and the one after that.

*Maybe your version is the doorway freeze, the birthday party greeting line, or the "she used to be so friendly" comment from a grandparent.* So let's walk through what's actually happening in a child's brain in that moment, what the research on temperament says, and the specific things that help — not overnight, but over time.

## Why Won't My Child Say Hi to People They Know?

Most of the time, it's not about the person. It's about the transition. A child who is playing, or riding in a car seat, or half-asleep from a long drive, gets dropped into a room full of adult energy — voices, hugs, expectations — with zero warning and zero runway.

Psychologist Jerome Kagan's long-running research on temperament found that somewhere between 15 and 20% of children are born with what he called a "high-reactive" nervous system — more physiologically sensitive to new people, new sounds, new rooms. These aren't kids with a problem. They're kids whose amygdala fires a little faster than average when something unfamiliar shows up, even if the "unfamiliar" thing is a relative they've technically met several times.

Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess's earlier temperament research (the New York Longitudinal Study, one of the most cited works in developmental psychology) put a name to this pattern decades before Kagan: "slow-to-warm-up" temperament. Not shy in the sense of a personality flaw. Slow. As in, needs time before engaging, and does fine once that time is given.

A slow-to-warm-up child might stand at the edge of a playground for a while before joining, or not answer a new teacher's questions on day one but be chatting with them a few days later. Once you have a name for that pattern, the greeting freeze at family gatherings makes a lot more sense. It isn't about the relative. It's about zero runway.

## Is It Normal for Kids to Refuse to Greet Relatives?

Yes — and it's common enough that it has its own body of parenting research, not just anecdotes. Kids aren't being rude when they don't greet on command. They're regulating a nervous system that hasn't caught up to the social expectation yet.

Janet Lansbury, whose work I've leaned on a lot over the years, writes about this directly: manners aren't something you can install through a single correction in the moment. They're absorbed slowly, the same way language is absorbed — through repetition and modeling, not through being put on the spot in front of an audience. A child who freezes isn't failing a manners test. They're having a normal nervous-system response to being socially cornered.

What made this click for me wasn't a parenting book, actually — it was watching how Luna's Montessori classroom handled the exact same moment.

## What Does Montessori Say About Teaching Manners?

Montessori classrooms have a specific practice for this called Grace and Courtesy — and it's built almost entirely around rehearsal, not real-time correction. Teachers walk young children through greeting rituals during quiet, low-stakes moments: how to shake a hand, how to say "good morning," how to introduce yourself. The child practices the motion and the words when nobody is actually watching and nothing is on the line.

Then, when a real greeting moment comes up — a parent arriving, a visitor at the door — the child has already rehearsed the script in their body. Nobody's standing over them saying "go on, say it" while they perform live for an audience for the first time.

This is the opposite of how most of us handle it at family gatherings. We wait until the pressure is highest — Grandma's at the door, everyone's watching — and that's the exact moment we ask a three-year-old to execute a brand-new social skill under maximum scrutiny. Grace and Courtesy flips that: practice low-stakes, perform when ready, never force the performance.

## Why Does Forcing a Greeting Backfire?

Two things happen when you push a child to greet on the spot, and neither one gets you a kid who says hi more easily next time.

First, you're teaching compliance, not courtesy. A child who says "hi" because three adults are staring at them and won't stop until they do isn't learning social warmth — they're learning that adults will apply pressure until they perform. That's a very different lesson than genuine greeting, and it doesn't transfer to the next unfamiliar person.

Second, and this is the part that surprised me: the label sticks. Every time someone says "she's shy" out loud, in front of a child, it becomes a tiny bit more true. Kids absorb the narrative adults build about them. Say "shy" enough times as a fixed trait instead of "she needs a minute" as a temporary state, and you can watch a child start living inside that label by age four or five.

It's an easy mistake to make — introducing your child to new people with "she's shy, give her a second" before they've even had the chance to decide for themselves.

## What Can I Say Instead of "Say Hi"?

These are the shifts that tend to change things — gradually, not overnight.

**Narrate instead of prompt.** Instead of "Say hi," try saying, "She's saying hi in her own way today" — to the other adult, not to your child. It takes the pressure off your child to perform on command and signals to the room that this isn't a battle you're going to fight in front of everyone.

**Give a runway before the interaction.** If you know you're walking into a gathering, tell your child on the way: "When we get inside, people are going to want to say hi. You don't have to say anything back right away. You can just watch for a bit." Naming the upcoming social moment in advance, with permission to opt out, does more than any script fed to them in the doorway.

**Offer alternatives, not ultimatums.** A wave. A high five. Standing next to you while you say hi for both of you. Any of those count. The goal isn't verbal compliance — it's giving your child more than one door into the interaction.

**Acknowledge, don't apologize.** "It can feel like a lot when everyone wants to say hi at once" — said to your child, quietly, not as an announcement to the room — validates what they're feeling without making it a performance issue for the adults watching.

**Practice at low-stakes moments, the Montessori way.** At home, role-play greetings with stuffed animals, or practice waving at the mail carrier through the window. Not "this is a lesson," just normal play that happens to rehearse the exact motion your child freezes on with relatives.

None of this fixes the freeze overnight. What actually changes things isn't a script that makes a child perform faster — it's enough runway and enough rehearsal that they get there on their own timeline.

## How Do I Handle the Judgmental Relative in the Room?

This is honestly the harder part for a lot of parents, and I don't think there's a clean answer. What tends to work is a short, low-drama line said once, not defended: "She'll come around when she's ready — no rush." Said flatly, not apologetically, it usually ends the topic faster than trying to explain temperament theory in someone's kitchen doorway.

If a relative pushes past that — "just make her say it" — it's usually more useful to redirect than argue: "Want to show everyone your drawing instead?" Giving your child a different way to connect usually does more than any conversation about manners ever could.

## FAQ

**Q: Is refusing to say hi a sign of a bigger problem, like anxiety or autism?**

For most kids, no — it's ordinary temperament variation, and the "slow-to-warm-up" pattern shows up in roughly a third of children studied in Thomas and Chess's original research. It's worth a conversation with your pediatrician if the avoidance is extreme, doesn't ease with familiarity over repeated exposure, or comes with other developmental concerns. But an occasional freeze at gatherings, on its own, is not a red flag.

**Q: What if my child is fine with kids but freezes with adults specifically?**

Very common, and usually about power dynamics rather than social ability. Adults tend to lean down, use a louder greeting voice, and expect eye contact — all things that read as more intense to a small child than a peer casually walking up. Letting your child approach an adult at their own pace, rather than having the adult approach first, often resolves this on its own.

**Q: Should I explain to relatives ahead of time that my kid might not say hi?**

If it's a gathering with people who tend to comment, a quick heads-up before you arrive ("she might need a few minutes to warm up, please don't push it") can prevent the in-the-moment pressure altogether. It's not about apologizing for your child — it's about setting the room up so nobody puts her on the spot.

**Q: At what age should kids be expected to greet people on their own?**

There's no fixed age where this should just click. Kids with easier temperaments may greet comfortably by three or four; slow-to-warm-up kids may still need a warm-up period well into early elementary school, and that's within a normal range. Consistency in modeling matters more than the calendar.

**Q: Does letting my child skip the greeting teach them it's okay to be rude?**

No — there's a real difference between skipping a greeting and being unkind, and kids can absolutely learn the difference. Teaching warmth (waving, smiling, standing near someone) without demanding a verbal performance on command still builds social connection. It just doesn't force it on someone else's timeline.
