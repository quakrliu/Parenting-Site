**TL;DR:** BloomPath gets this question constantly — a child buries their face in a parent's leg instead of saying hi, and someone in the room calls it rude. It usually isn't rudeness. It's temperament, and forcing the greeting almost always makes it worse. Model the greeting yourself, narrate for your child instead of prompting them, give them a warm-up window before any interaction, and save the "manners talk" for a calm moment at home — not in front of the audience that triggered it.

Thanksgiving, a few years back, at my sister-in-law's house outside Portland. Luna was about three. My husband's aunt bent down, arms half-open, and said, "Hi Luna! You remember me, right?" Luna turned, grabbed two fistfuls of my jeans, and disappeared behind my leg like I was a piece of furniture she could climb inside.

The aunt laughed it off — "oh, she's shy" — but my mother-in-law didn't let it go so easily. "Luna, honey, say hi. It's not hard." A little sing-song, a little pointed. Luna pressed her face harder into my leg. I remember standing there doing the math in my head: do I make her do it and end this awkward moment, or do I let it drag on and look like I'm raising a kid with no manners?

I did the thing a lot of us do the first few times. I crouched down and said, "Luna, say hi to Auntie Carol," in my brightest voice. She shook her head, buried deeper. Now there were three adults staring at a three-year-old, waiting.

Nothing about that moment made her more likely to say hi. If anything, it bought her another six months of hiding at every gathering after that.

*BloomPath has heard some version of this story from more parents than almost any other single scenario — the doorway freeze, the birthday party greeting line, the "she used to be so friendly" comment from a grandparent.* So I want to walk through what was actually happening in Luna's brain in that moment, what the research on temperament says, and the specific things that changed for us — not overnight, but over about a year of doing this differently.

## Why Won't My Child Say Hi to People They Know?

Most of the time, it's not about the person. It's about the transition. A child who is playing, or riding in a car seat, or half-asleep from a long drive, gets dropped into a room full of adult energy — voices, hugs, expectations — with zero warning and zero runway.

Psychologist Jerome Kagan's long-running research on temperament found that somewhere between 15 and 20% of children are born with what he called a "high-reactive" nervous system — more physiologically sensitive to new people, new sounds, new rooms. These aren't kids with a problem. They're kids whose amygdala fires a little faster than average when something unfamiliar shows up, even if the "unfamiliar" thing is Auntie Carol, who they've technically met four times.

Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess's earlier temperament research (the New York Longitudinal Study, one of the most cited works in developmental psychology) put a name to this pattern decades before Kagan: "slow-to-warm-up" temperament. Not shy in the sense of a personality flaw. Slow. As in, needs time before engaging, and does fine once that time is given.

Luna was — still is, honestly — a slow-to-warm-up kid. She'd stand at the edge of a playground for ten minutes before joining. She wouldn't answer a new teacher's questions on day one but would be chatting with them by day three. Once I had a name for that pattern, the greeting freeze at family gatherings made a lot more sense. It wasn't about Auntie Carol. It was about zero runway.

## Is It Normal for Kids to Refuse to Greet Relatives?

Yes — and it's common enough that it has its own body of parenting research, not just anecdotes. Kids aren't being rude when they don't greet on command. They're regulating a nervous system that hasn't caught up to the social expectation yet.

Janet Lansbury, whose work I've leaned on a lot over the years, writes about this directly: manners aren't something you can install through a single correction in the moment. They're absorbed slowly, the same way language is absorbed — through repetition and modeling, not through being put on the spot in front of an audience. A child who freezes isn't failing a manners test. They're having a normal nervous-system response to being socially cornered.

What made this click for me wasn't a parenting book, actually — it was watching how Luna's Montessori classroom handled the exact same moment.

## What Does Montessori Say About Teaching Manners?

Montessori classrooms have a specific practice for this called Grace and Courtesy — and it's built almost entirely around rehearsal, not real-time correction. Teachers walk young children through greeting rituals during quiet, low-stakes moments: how to shake a hand, how to say "good morning," how to introduce yourself. The child practices the motion and the words when nobody is actually watching and nothing is on the line.

Then, when a real greeting moment comes up — a parent arriving, a visitor at the door — the child has already rehearsed the script in their body. Nobody's standing over them saying "go on, say it" while they perform live for an audience for the first time.

This is the opposite of how most of us handle it at family gatherings. We wait until the pressure is highest — Grandma's at the door, everyone's watching — and that's the exact moment we ask a three-year-old to execute a brand-new social skill under maximum scrutiny. Grace and Courtesy flips that: practice low-stakes, perform when ready, never force the performance.

## Why Does Forcing a Greeting Backfire?

Two things happen when you push a child to greet on the spot, and neither one gets you a kid who says hi more easily next time.

First, you're teaching compliance, not courtesy. A child who says "hi" because three adults are staring at them and won't stop until they do isn't learning social warmth — they're learning that adults will apply pressure until they perform. That's a very different lesson than genuine greeting, and it doesn't transfer to the next unfamiliar person.

Second, and this is the part that surprised me: the label sticks. Every time someone said "she's shy" out loud, in front of Luna, it became a tiny bit more true. Kids absorb the narrative adults build about them. Say "shy" enough times as a fixed trait instead of "she needs a minute" as a temporary state, and you can watch a child start living inside that label by age four or five.

I made this mistake for about a year before I caught myself doing it — introducing Luna to new people with "she's shy, give her a second" before she'd even had the chance to decide for herself.

## What Can I Say Instead of "Say Hi"?

This is the part that actually changed things for us, gradually, over the following year or so.

**Narrate instead of prompt.** Instead of "Luna, say hi," I started saying, "Luna's saying hi in her own way today" — to the other adult, not to her. It took the pressure off her to perform on command and signaled to the room that this wasn't a battle I was going to fight in front of everyone.

**Give a runway before the interaction.** If we knew we were walking into a gathering, I'd tell Luna in the car: "When we get inside, people are going to want to say hi. You don't have to say anything back right away. You can just watch for a bit." Naming the upcoming social moment in advance, with permission to opt out, did more than any script I ever fed her in the doorway.

**Offer alternatives, not ultimatums.** A wave. A high five. Standing next to me while I said hi for both of us. Any of those counted. The goal wasn't verbal compliance — it was giving her more than one door into the interaction.

**Acknowledge, don't apologize.** "It can feel like a lot when everyone wants to say hi at once" — said to her, quietly, not as an announcement to the room — validated what she was feeling without making it a performance issue for the adults watching.

**Practice at low-stakes moments, the Montessori way.** At home, we'd role-play greetings with stuffed animals, or practice waving at the mail carrier through the window. Not "this is a lesson," just normal play that happened to rehearse the exact motion she froze on with relatives.

None of this fixed the freeze overnight. Luna was still quiet at gatherings through most of preschool. But somewhere around kindergarten, I noticed she'd started initiating the wave herself, unprompted, before anyone even said hi to her first. That's what actually changed — not a script that made her perform faster, but enough runway and enough rehearsal that she got there on her own timeline.

## How Do I Handle the Judgmental Relative in the Room?

This is honestly the harder part for a lot of parents, and I don't think there's a clean answer. What worked for us was a short, low-drama line said once, not defended: "She'll come around when she's ready — no rush." Said flatly, not apologetically, it usually ended the topic faster than trying to explain temperament theory in someone's kitchen doorway.

If a relative pushes past that — "just make her say it" — I found it more useful to redirect than argue: "Luna, want to show Auntie Carol your drawing instead?" Giving her a different way to connect usually did more than any conversation about manners ever could.

## FAQ

**Q: Is refusing to say hi a sign of a bigger problem, like anxiety or autism?**

For most kids, no — it's ordinary temperament variation, and the "slow-to-warm-up" pattern shows up in roughly a third of children studied in Thomas and Chess's original research. It's worth a conversation with your pediatrician if the avoidance is extreme, doesn't ease with familiarity over repeated exposure, or comes with other developmental concerns. But an occasional freeze at gatherings, on its own, is not a red flag.

**Q: What if my child is fine with kids but freezes with adults specifically?**

Very common, and usually about power dynamics rather than social ability. Adults tend to lean down, use a louder greeting voice, and expect eye contact — all things that read as more intense to a small child than a peer casually walking up. Letting your child approach an adult at their own pace, rather than having the adult approach first, often resolves this on its own.

**Q: Should I explain to relatives ahead of time that my kid might not say hi?**

If it's a gathering with people who tend to comment, a quick heads-up before you arrive ("she might need a few minutes to warm up, please don't push it") can prevent the in-the-moment pressure altogether. It's not about apologizing for your child — it's about setting the room up so nobody puts her on the spot.

**Q: At what age should kids be expected to greet people on their own?**

There's no fixed age where this should just click. Kids with easier temperaments may greet comfortably by three or four; slow-to-warm-up kids may still need a warm-up period well into early elementary school, and that's within a normal range. Consistency in modeling matters more than the calendar.

**Q: Does letting my child skip the greeting teach them it's okay to be rude?**

No — there's a real difference between skipping a greeting and being unkind, and kids can absolutely learn the difference. Teaching warmth (waving, smiling, standing near someone) without demanding a verbal performance on command still builds social connection. It just doesn't force it on someone else's timeline.
