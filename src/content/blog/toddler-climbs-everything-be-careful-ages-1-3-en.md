It's Saturday morning and you've just poured your coffee. You turn around and your 2-year-old is standing on the arm of the couch, one hand on the windowsill, reaching for the curtain rod. "Be careful!" comes out of your mouth before you've even put the mug down. You scoop them off. Ninety seconds later they've dragged a dining chair to the kitchen counter and are halfway up.

At the playground it's the same story. The ladder labeled for ages 5 to 12, the outside of the tube slide, the top of the picnic table. You follow them around with your arms half-raised, saying "careful, careful, careful" until the words stop meaning anything to either of you.

If that's your week, this BloomPath guide is for you. It's written for parents of children aged 1 to 3 who climb everything in sight. You'll find why toddlers are driven to climb, a quick way to sort the climbs you must stop from the ones worth allowing, words that work better than "be careful," and how Montessori and positive parenting turn a reckless climber into a careful one.

## Quick Answer

Climbing is a normal, healthy drive between 1 and 3. The CDC's 18-month milestone checklist includes climbing on and off a couch or chair without help. Your job is to sort climbs into two groups. **Stop every climb where a fall would be serious or where furniture can tip**: windows, balconies, dressers, bookshelves, anything near a hard edge with real height. **Allow and gently spot climbs at a height your child chose and can get down from**: the couch, a low playground structure, a step stool. Replace "be careful" with specific words ("feet on the step," "hold with two hands," "how will you get down?"), anchor heavy furniture to the wall, and give your child a safe place to climb every day. A child who practices climbing tends to fall less, and a child who is constantly stopped often climbs in secret.

## Why does my toddler climb everything?

Your toddler climbs because their body is wiring itself for balance, strength, and depth perception, and climbing is the fastest way to practice all three at once. Between 1 and 3, children are moving from wobbly walking to running, stepping up, and getting down. Every climb is a set of repetitions.

There's also plain curiosity. The counter is where the interesting things happen. The windowsill is where the birds are. From your toddler's point of view, height is the shortest path to the action.

And there's the drive Montessori called independence. A toddler who can get onto the couch by themselves no longer has to wait to be lifted. You'll often see the same child who screams "I do it myself!" at the shoe rack also refusing your hand at the playground ladder. It's the same stage. (We wrote about that stage in [the "I do it myself!" toddler guide](/en/blog/toddler-insists-do-it-myself-ages-1-3-en).)

I'll be honest: when our daughter was younger, I said "be careful" so often that it turned into background noise. For her and for me. It took me a long time to notice how little information those two words carried.

## Which climbs should I stop, and which should I allow?

Stop any climb where a fall would land your child on something hard from real height, and any climb on furniture that could tip. Allow climbs where your child chose the height, can get down on their own, and would land on a soft or low surface.

A simple way to decide in the moment is to ask yourself three questions:

1. **If they fall right now, what do they land on, and from how high?** A carpet from couch height is a bump. A tile floor from counter height is a different story.
2. **Can the thing they're climbing move or tip?** Dressers, bookshelves, TV stands, and stacked chairs are hard no's.
3. **Did they get up there by themselves?** Children who climb up on their own usually have the strength and coordination to get down. Children who were lifted onto something tall often don't.

| Usually stop | Usually allow (and stay close) |
|---|---|
| Windowsills, balcony railings, anything near an open window | Couch, ottoman, a low bed |
| Dressers, bookshelves, TV stands | Step stool or learning tower |
| Stacked chairs, wobbly furniture | Low playground climbers, small slides |
| Kitchen counters near the stove or knives | Low rocks, logs, small hills outdoors |
| Big-kid structures they need to be lifted onto | A Pikler-style climbing triangle on a soft mat |

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission's Anchor It campaign recommends anchoring TVs, dressers, and bookcases to the wall in any home where young children live or visit. CPSC data show that most children killed in tip-over incidents are under 5. That one afternoon with a drill does more for your toddler's safety than a thousand "careful"s.

## What should I say instead of "be careful"?

Say what you want your child to notice or do. "Be careful" tells a toddler that something is wrong, without telling them what. Specific words give them information they can act on.

Try these instead:

- **Point to the body:** "Feet on the yellow step." "Hold on with two hands."
- **Point to the surroundings:** "The rock is wet. Feel it with your hand first." "There's a big kid coming down the slide."
- **Ask a question that makes them think:** "How are you going to get down?" "Is that branch strong enough?"
- **Describe what you see:** "You climbed all the way to the third step." (This replaces "good job" and builds the same confidence.)
- **When you need to stop it, say so plainly:** "I won't let you stand on the windowsill. You can climb the couch cushions instead."

The last one matters. Specific words are for allowed climbs. For dangerous ones, you calmly move your child down and offer a yes. No lecture needed. Janet Lansbury's phrasing, "I won't let you," works well here because it tells your child that you're in charge of the limit, so they don't have to fight you for it.

## Is risky play actually good for toddlers?

Yes, within limits. Researchers use "risky play" to mean play with some uncertainty and a chance of a minor bump, like climbing, balancing, and going fast. It does not mean genuinely dangerous situations.

A 2015 systematic review led by Mariana Brussoni in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* looked at 21 studies and found that risky outdoor play was linked to more physical activity and better social behavior, and that most related injuries were minor. Norwegian researcher Ellen Sandseter has proposed that climbing and other thrilling play may help children work through normal fears of heights, a hypothesis she calls the "anti-phobic" effect.

There's also research on the other side of the coin. A 2007 meta-analysis by McLeod, Wood, and Weisz in *Clinical Psychology Review* found a modest but consistent link between high parental control (including overprotection) and anxiety in children. That doesn't mean saying "careful" causes anxiety. It does suggest that a child who is constantly rescued may learn that the world is scarier than it is, and that they can't handle it.

My take after years of watching kids at our daughter's school and at the playground: the children who climb most confidently are rarely the ones whose parents hover. They're the ones who've had hundreds of small chances to practice.

## What does Montessori say about toddlers and climbing?

Montessori says children develop through movement and that adults should prepare the environment so that the safe choice is also the interesting one. This is often called "freedom within limits." Your toddler gets real freedom to move, inside boundaries you've already made safe.

In practice, that looks like:

- **Give climbing a "yes" place.** A low climbing triangle, a sturdy step stool, a pile of floor cushions, or a mattress on the floor. If climbing has a home, you'll spend less time pulling your toddler off the bookshelf. (The Pikler triangle comes from Hungarian pediatrician Emmi Pikler, whose approach is close to Montessori: let children reach motor skills on their own timeline.)
- **Don't lift them onto things they can't climb themselves.** If your child can't get up alone, they usually can't get down alone either. Let their own body decide how high they go.
- **Prepare the room before you relax.** Anchor furniture, move chairs away from windows and counters, put window guards or stops on upstairs windows, and keep a soft surface under the climbing spot.
- **Use a learning tower for the kitchen.** If your toddler keeps dragging chairs to the counter, they're telling you they want to help. A learning tower gives them a safe way to see what you're doing.

Positive parenting adds the connection piece. When your child gets a scrape, sit with them for a moment ("That hurt. Your knee hit the step.") before jumping to fix or warn. A child who feels supported after a small fall is more willing to try again carefully.

## How do I stay calm when my toddler is climbing high?

Stand close, keep your hands ready but off, and breathe out slowly. Your body language is information for your toddler. If you gasp and grab, they learn that climbing is frightening. If you stay steady, they learn they can concentrate.

A few things help:

- **Spot, don't hold.** Stand where you could catch them, with your hands near but not on their body.
- **Watch their feet, not their face.** It keeps you focused on what's actually happening.
- **Count to five before you speak.** Most wobbles resolve on their own.
- **Pick your one rule for each place.** At the playground it might be "only climb what you can get onto by yourself." Fewer rules, said calmly, get followed more.

Ethan is better at this than I am. Years in, I still catch myself tensing up. The difference now is that I try to keep the tension in my shoulders and out of my voice.

## What if my toddler is the opposite and won't climb at all?

Some toddlers are cautious, and that's normal too. Don't push or lift them up. Make low climbing easy and available, climb near them yourself, and describe small brave moments ("You stepped onto the cushion"). If your child is not walking by 18 months, or seems to lose motor skills they had before, talk to your pediatrician.

If your cautious child also gives up quickly on puzzles and new tasks, [this guide on preschoolers who say "I can't"](/en/blog/preschooler-says-i-cant-gives-up-easily-en) has ideas that apply to younger kids too.

## When should I call a doctor?

Call your doctor or seek emergency care after any fall where your child loses consciousness, vomits repeatedly, seems unusually sleepy or confused, can't move a limb normally, or cries in a way that won't settle. Falls from windows and tipping furniture can cause serious injuries in young children, which is why the "stop" list above matters more than any of the climbing tips.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal for a 2-year-old to climb everything?**
Yes. Climbing is a common, healthy drive between 1 and 3. The CDC lists climbing on and off a couch or chair without help on its 18-month milestone checklist. Climbing builds balance, strength, and coordination.

**How do I stop my toddler from climbing furniture?**
Anchor tall furniture to the wall, move chairs away from counters and windows, and calmly take your child down from unsafe spots with a clear limit ("I won't let you climb the shelf"). Then offer a safe place to climb, like a couch cushion pile or a climbing triangle.

**What can I say instead of "be careful"?**
Use specific words: "Feet on the step," "Hold with two hands," "How will you get down?" or "That rock is wet." Specific cues tell your child what to notice, while "be careful" only tells them something is wrong.

**Is risky play safe for toddlers?**
Risky play means play with uncertainty and a chance of minor bumps, such as climbing and balancing, inside a safe setting. A 2015 systematic review found it was linked to more physical activity and that most related injuries were minor. Genuine hazards like windows, balconies, and tipping furniture should always be off-limits.

**What does Montessori say about climbing?**
Montessori views movement as central to development and recommends preparing the environment so children can move freely within safe limits. At home, that means a dedicated climbing spot, anchored furniture, and letting children climb only as high as they can get by themselves.

**Should I help my toddler climb the big playground equipment?**
Usually no. If you lift your child onto something they couldn't reach alone, they may not have the strength or skill to get down. Let them climb only what they can get onto by themselves, and stay close to spot.
