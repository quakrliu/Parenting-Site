It's Tuesday, a little after ten. You go to your 14-year-old's room to remind them about the permission slip, and their laptop is open on the desk while they're in the shower. You don't mean to read it. But the chat window is right there, and the last message says: *"I don't think anyone at school actually likes me. I can't tell my mom, she'd make it a whole thing."* Above it, a long scroll. Weeks of conversations. About a friend who stopped sitting with them. About feeling stupid in math. About you.

If your stomach dropped reading that, BloomPath wrote this for you. You're probably feeling two things at once: relief that your kid is talking to *something*, and a sting that it isn't you. Both are fair. And the next 48 hours matter more than the discovery itself.

**TL;DR:** A teen confiding in an AI chatbot usually means they want a judgment-free place to think out loud. It rarely means they've given up on you. Don't confiscate or interrogate. Name what you saw calmly, lead with curiosity, lower the stakes of telling you things, and make a shared plan for which topics need a real human. If the chats mention self-harm, hopelessness, or an adult contacting them, act the same day.

## Why would my teen tell an AI instead of me?

Most teens turn to a chatbot because it can't react. It won't sigh, won't email the teacher, won't bring it up at dinner, and won't remember it next week in the middle of an argument. For a 12-to-15-year-old, that matters enormously.

The numbers say you're far from alone. Common Sense Media's 2025 report *Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs*, a survey of about 1,060 U.S. teens, found that 72% had tried an AI companion at least once, and about a third of the teens who used them had chosen to discuss something important or serious with the AI instead of a real person. Roughly 31% said those conversations felt as satisfying as, or more satisfying than, talking with friends. (The researchers' own framing is worth keeping in mind: most teens still said human conversations were better.)

Here's my engineer's read on it. A chatbot is a system with zero latency and zero consequences. You type, it answers, nothing leaks. What your teen is really picking is *the absence of consequences*. That's a design problem you can actually work on, because it tells you which consequences they're afraid of.

## What is going on in a 12-to-15-year-old's brain?

The short answer: they're wired to pull away from parents and toward peers, and wired to feel embarrassment at full volume. Daniel Siegel, in *Brainstorm*, describes adolescence as a period of heightened emotional intensity, social engagement, and novelty seeking. Those traits are part of healthy development. They also explain why a teen will rehearse a hard feeling privately before risking saying it out loud to someone whose opinion matters.

Maria Montessori wrote about adolescents needing something she called valorization: the experience of being treated as a capable person whose thinking counts. She argued that teens shut down when adults handle them like younger children and open up when they're given real responsibility and respect. A chatbot, oddly, delivers a cheap version of that. It takes every sentence seriously. It never says "you're overreacting."

Mei put it to me this way after reading through some of the Montessori adolescent material: the AI is giving our kids the respect part without the relationship part. Our job is to offer both.

## Should I take the phone or block the app?

Not as your first move. Confiscating the device the night you discover the chats teaches one lesson clearly: *telling the truth anywhere leads to losing things.* The next conversation goes somewhere you can't see at all.

There are legitimate reasons to tighten controls, and we'll get to them. But in the first conversation, the device stays. You're trying to find out what your teen was working through, and that information disappears the moment the topic becomes "punishment."

I'll be honest: my first instinct in situations like this is still to fix the system. Lock it down, add a filter, ship a patch. Years in, I still have to remind myself that with a teenager, the relationship *is* the system.

## What should I say the night I find out?

Say less than you want to, and say it calmly. You're aiming for a 3-minute opener, not a 40-minute summit. Here's a sequence that works for a lot of families:

1. **Own how you saw it.** "When I came in to grab the permission slip, your laptop was open and I read some of the chat. I should have closed it. I'm telling you because I don't want to have a secret about it."
2. **Lead with what you felt for them, not about you.** "I read the part where you said nobody at school likes you. That sounded really lonely, and I'm sorry you've been carrying it."
3. **Name the hard part without blame.** "I also saw that you didn't want to tell me because I'd make it a whole thing. That's fair feedback. I do sometimes jump in too fast."
4. **Offer a choice, not a demand.** "You don't have to talk about it tonight. If you ever want to, I'll just listen first. No fixing, no telling anyone."
5. **Leave.** Actually walk out. The door being open matters more than the conversation finishing.

Dr. Becky Kennedy talks about using the "most generous interpretation" of a child's behavior. The most generous read here: your teen found a safe-feeling way to process something painful. That's a coping skill. You're trying to widen it, not punish it.

**Words to avoid that night:** "Why would you tell a robot and not me?" "Do you know who reads those?" "I'm your mother." Each one makes the conversation about your hurt, which is exactly what your teen predicted would happen.

## How do I become someone my teen actually talks to?

You lower the cost of telling you things, one small conversation at a time. Teens test with low-stakes material before they bring anything heavy. If a complaint about a teacher gets a lecture, the friend drama never arrives.

A few habits that help:

- **Side-by-side beats face-to-face.** Car rides, walking the dog, cooking. Less eye contact, lower pressure. Many parents notice the real stuff comes out at 10 p.m. in the kitchen, not at the scheduled "check-in."
- **Ask "do you want help or do you want me to listen?"** Then do exactly what they choose. This one question repairs a lot.
- **Keep confidences.** If your teen tells you about a friend's problem and you repeat it to another parent, you've closed the channel for a year. Safety issues are the exception, and you should say so up front.
- **Repair out loud.** When you overreact, come back: "I got loud about the grade earlier. That was about my worry, not about you." Repair teaches teens that a rough reaction isn't the end of the relationship.
- **Let them be the expert.** Ask them to show you the chatbot. What do they like about it? What does it get wrong? A teen who gets to explain something to you is a teen who's talking to you.

In my opinion, that last one is the most underrated move here. Most of us approach AI tools with suspicion. Your teen has probably already noticed its limits. Asking about them invites the critical thinking you want, without a lecture.

## Which conversations should go to a person, not a chatbot?

Some topics need a human, and your teen can help you write that list. The American Psychological Association's 2025 health advisory on AI and adolescent well-being urges parents to remind teens that AI health information is no substitute for a professional and should be checked with a trusted adult or a health care provider.

Make a short "human list" together. Ours looks something like this:

- Thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive
- Anyone, online or off, asking for photos, secrets, or to meet up
- Physical symptoms, medication, eating, or sleep problems lasting weeks
- Feeling unsafe at school or at home

Then give your teen at least two humans for that list, and you don't have to be one of them. An aunt, a school counselor, a coach. The goal is a teen who has *people*, and a chatbot that sits below them in the stack.

## When should I worry about what I read?

Act the same day if the chats include hopelessness, self-harm, wanting to disappear, an adult or stranger asking for secrecy, or the AI encouraging risky behavior. Stay calm, stay with your teen, and get help: in the U.S., call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), and contact your pediatrician or a mental health professional. If there's immediate danger, call 911.

Short of an emergency, watch for patterns over weeks: dropping friendships, pulling away from activities they used to enjoy, big changes in sleep or appetite, or hours of nightly conversation with an AI that seems to replace human contact rather than supplement it. That's when tighter limits and a professional opinion make sense, and you can explain them as care, not punishment. If you're unsure what counts as normal teen moodiness, our guide to [how AI companion apps are designed](/en/blog/kids-ai-companion-chatbot-safety-en) covers the warning signs on the tech side.

## What about my own reaction?

It's normal to feel replaced, and it's worth sitting with that before you talk to your teen. Think of the chatbot as a mirror showing you which conversations your kid doesn't yet feel safe having with you.

That's useful information, even if it stings. Mei reminded me that we got this wrong many times too, in smaller ways: a homework question that turned into a lecture, a story about a friend that we "fixed" before it was finished. Those moments add up. So do the repairs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it bad if my teenager talks to an AI chatbot about their feelings?**
Not by itself. Many teens use chatbots to think through feelings privately. It becomes a concern when the AI replaces human support, when the topics involve self-harm or safety, or when the app encourages secrecy or risky behavior.

**How common is it for teens to confide in AI?**
Common Sense Media's 2025 survey of about 1,060 U.S. teens found 72% had tried an AI companion, and about a third of teen users had discussed something serious with the AI instead of a real person.

**Should I read my teen's AI chats?**
If you've already seen them, tell your teen honestly. Routine secret reading usually damages trust. If you have a specific safety concern, say so directly and explain why you need to look.

**Should I ban AI chatbots for my 13-year-old?**
A total ban often pushes use out of sight. Many families do better with shared rules: which apps, where devices charge overnight, and a list of topics that always go to a trusted human.

**How do I get my teenager to talk to me instead?**
Lower the cost of telling you things. Ask whether they want help or just listening, keep confidences unless safety is involved, talk side by side in the car or kitchen, and repair out loud when you overreact.

**When is a teen's AI use a sign I should get help?**
Act the same day if chats mention self-harm, hopelessness, or an adult asking for secrecy; in the U.S., call or text 988. Over weeks, watch for withdrawal from friends, sleep or appetite changes, or AI use replacing human contact, and talk to your pediatrician.
