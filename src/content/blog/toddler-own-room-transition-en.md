We'd room-shared long past the point most sleep books consider "normal." Not because of any grand parenting philosophy — mostly because our place was small, she woke up if we breathed wrong, and moving her felt like defusing a bomb every single night. Then last month we finally did it. It took about three weeks, it was messier than the blog posts promised, and here's exactly what we learned.

**TL;DR:** There's no required age for a toddler to move into their own room — the [AAP](https://www.aap.org/) only requires room-sharing for the first six months for SIDS-risk reasons. After that, readiness (not a birthday) matters most: can your child fall asleep without contact, and stay in bed instead of climbing out? We used a staged, three-week approach — naps first, then the bedtime routine, then full nights — plus a low [Montessori floor bed](/en/blog/montessori-at-home-guide-en) that let our daughter get in and out on her own. Occasional 2 AM comebacks are normal and don't undo the progress.

## When Should a Toddler Move to Their Own Room?

There's no single correct age, but there is a research-backed floor. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends room-sharing (not bed-sharing) for at least the first six months, ideally closer to twelve, to reduce SIDS risk. After that window closes, the "right" time becomes much more about your specific kid and household than any calendar date.

What we found more useful than an age cutoff was a readiness checklist: Can she fall asleep with you nearby but not touching her? Does she stay in a big-kid bed instead of climbing out constantly? Is she sleeping through most naps without you in the room? We didn't hit all three until closer to three years old, and that's genuinely fine — especially if [bedtime stalling](/en/blog/toddler-bedtime-stalling-keeps-getting-up-en) or fear of the dark is still part of your nightly routine. A lot of parenting content implies there's a deadline. There mostly isn't one — there's just a moment when it gets easier than not doing it.

## Is Waiting Too Long Bad for Attachment?

No — and this is the guilt trip I want to talk you down from, because I fell into it hard. Secure attachment is built through consistent, responsive care during the day and in the moments your child needs you, not through the specific location of a mattress. Sleeping separately at eighteen months doesn't create more independence than sleeping separately at three; it just changes which weeks are hard.

Mei's the one who actually reads the parenting research in our house, and she pointed me toward how Montessori educators think about a child's environment: independence isn't something you install by removing the parent — it's something you build by making the environment safe enough for the child to succeed without needing rescue every ten minutes. That reframing helped more than any schedule did, and it's the same one that eventually talked my own [in-laws down from their objections](/en/blog/grandparents-dont-follow-parenting-rules-en) about the whole idea. A floor bed, low enough that she can get in and out herself, wasn't about pushing her away. It was about giving her a space she could actually manage on her own terms.

## What Actually Helped Us Make the Switch?

We didn't do this in one night, and I'd stop anyone who tried to. Here's the sequence that worked for us, roughly one week per stage:

1. **Naps first, nights later.** For the first week, she only slept in her own room for daytime naps, with the door open and one of us nearby. This let her get used to the space with the stakes low — a bad nap is annoying, a bad 2 AM night is a crisis.
2. **Move the whole bedtime routine into the new room.** Books, the same two songs, same order, every night — but physically happening in her room instead of ours. By the second week, the room itself had started to mean "sleep," not just the routine.
3. **Let her choose one thing.** She picked her own pillowcase — a dinosaur print she still talks about — and a small clip-on light she could turn on herself if she woke up. That one decision mattered more than I expected. It wasn't about the pillowcase. It was about her having a say in a change that otherwise felt like it was happening *to* her.
4. **Stay, but stay boring.** For the first several nights, one of us sat in a chair near the door — not in the bed, not chatting, just present — and moved the chair a little further out every two or three nights. Boring presence, it turns out, is different from co-sleeping. She could see we were there without needing us in the bed to fall asleep.

My friend Dave, whose son is a few months older than my daughter, went through this around the same time, and comparing notes turned out to be more useful than any article I read. His approach was the opposite of ours — full cry-it-out in one weekend — and it worked for his kid. I mention this because I spent a lot of energy worrying we were doing it "wrong" by going slower, and the honest answer is there isn't one correct method. There's just the one that matches your kid's temperament and your own tolerance for a rough week.

## What About the Nights She Comes Back?

She still does, probably twice a month, usually after a rough day at school or a cold. We stopped treating this as a failure of the transition and started treating it as information — she's telling us something feels off, and a return trip to snuggle for ten minutes doesn't undo three weeks of progress. What actually would undo it is panic on our part, treating every setback like we're starting over from zero.

One night about two weeks in, she showed up in our doorway at 1 AM, silent, just standing there. I almost said "back to bed" on autopilot. Instead I walked her back, laid down next to her floor bed (not in it) for about four minutes, and left. She was asleep before I stood up. That one small choice — presence without permanence — ended up being the whole strategy in miniature.

## Does a Montessori Floor Bed Actually Make This Easier?

For us, yes, though I want to be careful not to oversell it. The Montessori approach to sleep isn't a brand of mattress — it's the idea that a low bed a toddler can get into and out of independently supports the same self-reliance Montessori classrooms build during the day. She could get up if she woke up thirsty instead of needing to call out and wait. She could climb back in on her own after a bathroom trip instead of needing to be lifted. Small things, but they removed a lot of the friction that used to turn a 3 AM wake-up into a fifteen-minute negotiation.

It's not a magic fix for a toddler who genuinely isn't ready. If your child is still climbing out of the crib at random hours or waking up in a full panic, a floor bed alone won't solve that — you may want to revisit some of what we found out about [why toddlers wake up screaming during sleep regressions](/en/blog/toddler-sleep-regression-guide-en) — or about [what actually helps with fear of the dark](/en/blog/toddler-afraid-of-dark-bedtime-fears-en) — before tackling the room move at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What age should a toddler move to their own room?**
There's no single required age. The AAP recommends room-sharing for at least six months for SIDS-risk reasons, but beyond that, readiness matters more than a birthday. Look for whether your child can fall asleep without physical contact and stays in bed instead of climbing out repeatedly.

**Is it bad to keep room-sharing past age two?**
No. Room-sharing past two years old is common and not linked to worse outcomes. Secure attachment comes from responsive daytime and nighttime care, not from the specific sleeping arrangement.

**How long does the transition to a toddler's own room usually take?**
For us it took about three weeks using a staged approach — naps first, then the bedtime routine, then full nights. Some kids adjust in days; others need six to eight weeks. Both are normal.

**What is a Montessori floor bed and does it help?**
A Montessori floor bed is a low mattress a toddler can get in and out of independently. It supports the same self-reliance Montessori environments encourage during the day, and can reduce nighttime friction, though it's not a substitute for readiness.

**What should we do if our toddler keeps coming back to our room after the move?**
Treat occasional returns as information, not failure. A brief, calm return to their bed — without turning it into a long negotiation or a permanent invitation back into your bed — usually keeps the overall transition on track.
