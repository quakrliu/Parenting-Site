If you've ever spent bedtime lying on the floor of your toddler's room with one arm through the crib-turned-toddler-bed rails, because that's the only way they'll stay horizontal — back aching, phone battery nearly dead — you've probably wondered whether you're doing this transition right.

Maybe you've room-shared long past the point most sleep books consider "normal." Not because of any grand parenting philosophy — maybe your place is small, your child wakes up if you breathe wrong, and moving them feels like defusing a bomb every single night. When you're ready to make the switch, it can take a few weeks, and it's often messier than the blog posts promise. Here's what helps.

**TL;DR:** There's no required age for a toddler to move into their own room — the [AAP](https://www.aap.org/) recommends room-sharing for at least the first six months for SIDS-risk reasons. After that, readiness (not a birthday) matters most: can your child fall asleep without contact, and stay in bed instead of climbing out? Try a staged approach — naps first, then the bedtime routine, then full nights — plus a low [Montessori floor bed](/en/blog/montessori-at-home-guide-en) that lets your child get in and out on their own. Occasional 2 AM comebacks are normal and don't undo the progress.

## When Should a Toddler Move to Their Own Room?

There's no single correct age, but there is a research-backed floor. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends room-sharing (not bed-sharing) for at least the first six months to reduce SIDS risk. After that window closes, the "right" time becomes much more about your specific kid and household than any calendar date.

More useful than an age cutoff is a readiness checklist: Can your child fall asleep with you nearby but not touching them? Do they stay in a big-kid bed instead of climbing out constantly? Are they sleeping through most naps without you in the room? If your child doesn't hit all three until closer to three years old, that's genuinely fine — especially if [bedtime stalling](/en/blog/toddler-bedtime-stalling-keeps-getting-up-en) or fear of the dark is still part of your nightly routine. A lot of parenting content implies there's a deadline. There mostly isn't one — there's just a moment when it gets easier than not doing it.

## Is Waiting Too Long Bad for Attachment?

No — and this is the guilt trip I want to talk you down from, because I fell into it hard. Secure attachment is built through consistent, responsive care during the day and in the moments your child needs you, not through the specific location of a mattress. Sleeping separately at eighteen months doesn't create more independence than sleeping separately at three; it just changes which weeks are hard.

Mei's the one who actually reads the parenting research in our house, and she pointed me toward how Montessori educators think about a child's environment: independence isn't something you install by removing the parent — it's something you build by making the environment safe enough for the child to succeed without needing rescue every ten minutes. That reframing can help more than any schedule does, and it's a useful one to share if [grandparents object](/en/blog/grandparents-dont-follow-parenting-rules-en) to the whole idea. A floor bed, low enough that a child can get in and out on their own, isn't about pushing them away. It's about giving them a space they can actually manage on their own terms.

## What Actually Helps Make the Switch?

I wouldn't try to do this in one night. Here's a staged sequence to try, roughly one week per stage:

1. **Naps first, nights later.** For the first week, your child only sleeps in their own room for daytime naps, with the door open and a parent nearby. This lets them get used to the space with the stakes low — a bad nap is annoying, a bad 2 AM night is a crisis.
2. **Move the whole bedtime routine into the new room.** Books, the same two songs, same order, every night — but physically happening in their room instead of yours. Over time, the room itself starts to mean "sleep," not just the routine.
3. **Let them choose one thing.** Maybe it's their own pillowcase, or a small clip-on light they can turn on themselves if they wake up. That one decision can matter more than you'd expect. It isn't about the pillowcase. It's about your child having a say in a change that otherwise feels like it's happening *to* them.
4. **Stay, but stay boring.** For the first several nights, sit in a chair near the door — not in the bed, not chatting, just present — and move the chair a little further out every two or three nights. Boring presence is different from co-sleeping. Your child can see you're there without needing you in the bed to fall asleep.

Other families go the opposite way — full cry-it-out in one weekend — and for some kids that works too. If you're worried you're doing it "wrong" by going slower, the honest answer is there isn't one correct method. There's just the one that matches your kid's temperament and your own tolerance for a rough week.

## What About the Nights They Come Back?

It happens — often after a rough day or a cold. Try not to treat this as a failure of the transition; treat it as information — your child is telling you something feels off, and a return trip to snuggle for ten minutes doesn't undo weeks of progress. What actually would undo it is panic, treating every setback like you're starting over from zero.

If your child shows up in your doorway at 1 AM, silent, just standing there, it's tempting to say "back to bed" on autopilot. Instead, try walking them back, lying down next to their bed (not in it) for a few minutes, and leaving. That one small choice — presence without permanence — is the whole strategy in miniature.

## Does a Montessori Floor Bed Actually Make This Easier?

It can, though I want to be careful not to oversell it. The Montessori approach to sleep isn't a brand of mattress — it's the idea that a low bed a toddler can get into and out of independently supports the same self-reliance Montessori classrooms build during the day. A child can get up if they wake up thirsty instead of needing to call out and wait, and climb back in on their own after a bathroom trip instead of needing to be lifted. Small things, but they remove a lot of the friction that can turn a 3 AM wake-up into a fifteen-minute negotiation.

It's not a magic fix for a toddler who genuinely isn't ready. If your child is still climbing out of the crib at random hours or waking up in a full panic, a floor bed alone won't solve that — you may want to revisit some of what we found out about [why toddlers wake up screaming during sleep regressions](/en/blog/toddler-sleep-regression-guide-en) — or about [what actually helps with fear of the dark](/en/blog/toddler-afraid-of-dark-bedtime-fears-en) — before tackling the room move at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What age should a toddler move to their own room?**
There's no single required age. The AAP recommends room-sharing for at least six months for SIDS-risk reasons, but beyond that, readiness matters more than a birthday. Look for whether your child can fall asleep without physical contact and stays in bed instead of climbing out repeatedly.

**Is it bad to keep room-sharing past age two?**
No. Room-sharing past two years old is common and not linked to worse outcomes. Secure attachment comes from responsive daytime and nighttime care, not from the specific sleeping arrangement.

**How long does the transition to a toddler's own room usually take?**
A staged approach — naps first, then the bedtime routine, then full nights — takes around three weeks at roughly a week per stage. Some kids adjust in days; others need six to eight weeks. Both are normal.

**What is a Montessori floor bed and does it help?**
A Montessori floor bed is a low mattress a toddler can get in and out of independently. It supports the same self-reliance Montessori environments encourage during the day, and can reduce nighttime friction, though it's not a substitute for readiness.

**What should we do if our toddler keeps coming back to our room after the move?**
Treat occasional returns as information, not failure. A brief, calm return to their bed — without turning it into a long negotiation or a permanent invitation back into your bed — usually keeps the overall transition on track.
