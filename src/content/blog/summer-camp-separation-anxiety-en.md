**TL;DR**
- Homesickness and drop-off crying at summer camp is the norm, not the exception — one widely cited camp study found roughly 83% of campers reported feeling homesick on at least one day.
- The two mistakes that make it worse: sneaking out without saying goodbye, and promising you'll come get them early if it's too hard.
- A short, consistent, boring goodbye ritual — the same words, same length, every time — works better than a long comforting one.
- Practicing short separations *before* camp starts (a sleepover at grandma's, a few hours at a friend's) builds the actual skill camp requires.
- Most kids settle within 2-3 days. If they're not eating, sleeping, or engaging with anyone by day 4-5, that's the signal to loop in the camp director or your pediatrician.

## Why does my kid cry at camp drop-off when they're usually fine at school?

Because camp is a different kind of unfamiliar than school, even for a kid who's been at the same school building for a year. New adults, new kids, no assigned seat, no idea where the bathroom is, and often a shorter runway to adjust — a week or two of camp instead of a full semester to build trust with a teacher.

Child Mind Institute researchers who study anxious kids at camp point out that separation anxiety is fundamentally a sign of a secure attachment doing its job — your kid's brain is telling them "the person who keeps me safe just left," which is a completely reasonable thing for a small nervous system to flag. It's not regression. It's not your kid being "too sensitive" for camp. It's the same wiring that makes a two-year-old cry when you leave the room, showing up again in a slightly bigger, more articulate body.

What's different at camp versus a slow, familiar drop-off routine is the compressed timeline. At preschool, you get months of the same hallway, the same cubby, the same teacher's face. At a one- or two-week day camp, your kid has to build that sense of "this place is safe" in about 48 hours, while you're also gone longer than usual and the routine is genuinely new. Mei's read enough of the developmental psych on this to point out that the anxiety isn't really about camp — it's about uncertainty, and camp is uncertainty wearing a lanyard.

## What actually makes separation anxiety at camp worse?

Two things, and I did both of them with our older camp drop-offs before we figured this out.

**Mistake one: the sneak-out.** It feels kind in the moment — slip away while they're distracted by the craft table, avoid the meltdown entirely. It backfires. The next day, your kid doesn't relax when they're distracted; they watch you like a hawk, because now leaving-without-saying-goodbye is a real possibility they have to guard against. You've traded one hard morning for a harder week.

**Mistake two: the escape hatch promise.** "If you hate it, I'll come get you at lunch." I get why this feels compassionate. Researchers who work with anxious campers specifically flag this one — telling a kid you'll rescue them early sends the message that homesickness is an emergency to be avoided rather than a feeling to move through. It also means every hard moment becomes a negotiation instead of something they just get through, which is exactly the skill camp is supposed to build.

The counterintuitive move that camp psychologists recommend instead: when your kid is homesick mid-day, the advice isn't to retreat and call you — it's to get them moving into a group activity. Sitting alone with the feeling makes it bigger. A game of gaga ball makes it smaller. You can prep your kid for this before day one: "If you feel sad, tell a counselor and ask what game is starting next," rather than "call me."

## How to handle toddler and preschooler separation anxiety differently at camp

For the 4-6 crowd, the goodbye ritual matters more than the pep talk. A three-year-old doesn't process "camp is going to be so fun, you'll make new friends" as reassurance — it processes tone of voice and how long you linger. What worked for us with Luna at that age wasn't a longer goodbye, it was a shorter one, done the exact same way every single day: one hug, the same phrase ("See you after lunch, I'll be right there"), then I walked away without looking back for a "one more hug."

If you want the deeper research on why routine beats reassurance for this age group, we broke down the [research on daily routines and separation anxiety](/en/blog/kindergarten-separation-anxiety-routines-research-en) — the short version is that predictability regulates a young nervous system more effectively than comfort does, because comfort is variable and routine isn't.

For kids heading into their first drop-off program altogether — not just camp, any new setting — [prepping before day one](/en/blog/toddler-preschool-separation-anxiety-prep-en) with small, deliberate practice separations makes a measurable difference by the time the real thing happens.

## How to handle school-age and overnight camp separation anxiety

Older kids (7-10) are dealing with something a little different: they're embarrassed to still be anxious. They know they're "too old to cry about camp," which means the anxiety often shows up sideways — a stomachache the morning of, sudden pickiness about what they're wearing, an argument with you in the car that has nothing to do with camp.

For this age, involving them in the prep actually helps more than for younger kids. Before overnight camp specifically, American Camp Association research on homesickness recommends walking through logistics concretely — where they'll sleep, what the schedule looks like, who to find if something's wrong — because uncertainty is doing a lot of the anxiety's work. A kid who can picture the cabin and knows there's a specific counselor named "Coach J" to find is dealing with a known quantity, not a void.

Practice separations before the real one also matters more at this age, not less. A sleepover at a grandparent's house or a weekend with a trusted family friend gives an 8-year-old actual reps at "I was away from my parents and I was okay" — which is a very different, more durable confidence than a pep talk the morning of.

One thing I got wrong with Luna before a longer program: I under-communicated how she could reach us. Once she knew exactly when letter-writing time was and that a letter back from us would be waiting a couple days later, the anxious mornings before drop-off dropped noticeably. Not eliminated — dropped.

## What does Montessori say about separation and independence?

Montessori environments are built around a core idea that's directly relevant here: children build confidence through mastering small, real challenges, not through being protected from them. A toddler pouring their own milk and spilling it, a five-year-old buttoning their own coat slowly while you wait — that's the same muscle as a camper getting through a homesick afternoon and discovering they can handle it.

That doesn't mean camp separation anxiety is something to tough out coldly. It means the goal isn't "prevent the hard feeling," it's "help your kid discover they can move through the hard feeling and come out the other side fine." That's a subtle but important difference, and it's the same philosophy behind our [guide to Montessori at home](/en/blog/montessori-at-home-guide-en) — competence comes from doing the hard, slightly uncomfortable thing, with someone steady nearby, not from having the hard thing removed.

## Our actual goodbye script (steal this)

Here's exactly what I say now, every drop-off, no variation:

1. One hug, no longer than five seconds.
2. "I love you. I'll be back after [specific, concrete time — lunch, swim time, 3 o'clock]. Have a great time."
3. Hand her off to a specific adult by name if possible: "Luna, go tell Coach J you're here."
4. Walk away. Don't turn around for a second wave, even if she's still upset. Turning around resets the goodbye and makes it longer, not shorter.

The boring, repetitive part is the entire point. A kid's nervous system doesn't calm down because the goodbye was eloquent — it calms down because the goodbye was predictable enough to stop needing to be monitored.

## When is separation anxiety at camp something more than normal?

Most kids settle in within 2-3 days. That's genuinely the average, not the best-case scenario. Give it a real 2-3 days before deciding camp isn't working.

Signs it's worth a conversation with the camp director or your pediatrician: your kid isn't eating meals by day 4-5, isn't sleeping (for overnight camp), is completely withdrawn from every activity rather than just quiet, or the anxiety is escalating instead of easing as the days go on. That pattern is different from garden-variety homesickness, and it's worth ruling out something more — a specific fear about the setting, a peer conflict, an anxiety disorder that camp is surfacing rather than causing.

I'll be honest about something I got wrong here too: with our first camp experience, I waited too long to ask the counselor how Luna's days were actually going, because I didn't want to be "that parent." Camp staff do this every summer. Ask. It took one two-minute conversation to find out she was completely fine by 10am every day — she just cried hard at the exact moment I was still watching.

## FAQ

**Is it normal for my child to cry every single day at camp drop-off, even in week two?**
It's common in the first few days, less common by week two. If crying is still happening at the same intensity into a second week with no improvement, that's worth a check-in with camp staff — it may mean the goodbye routine at home needs adjusting, or there's something specific happening at camp (a peer conflict, a schedule that's overwhelming) worth investigating.

**Should I stay and watch until my child calms down?**
No — this is one of the more counterintuitive findings. Lingering, even out of love, tends to prolong the crying because your presence signals that leaving is still negotiable. A quick, warm, consistent goodbye followed by an actual departure helps kids settle faster than a drawn-out one.

**My child seems fine at drop-off but melts down at pickup. What's going on?**
This is extremely common and it's actually a good sign, not a bad one — kids often hold it together all day at camp because they're using every bit of self-regulation they have, and then release it the second they see a safe person. It's not that camp went badly; it's that you're the person they trust enough to fall apart with.

**Is it different for kids who've never been away from a parent before versus kids with camp experience?**
Yes — first-time separation, in any setting, tends to be harder regardless of age, because the child doesn't yet have a stored memory of "I was away and it was okay." That's why practicing shorter separations (a few hours with a grandparent, a sleepover) before a first camp experience genuinely helps; it gives the brain a reference point to draw on.

**Is a comfort object like a stuffed animal or family photo okay to send to camp?**
Yes, most camps allow a small comfort item, and it's a legitimate, research-supported strategy — not something to be embarrassed about, at any age up through elementary school. A photo tucked in a backpack pocket or a small stuffed animal that stays in a cubby (not necessarily in active play) can genuinely ease the transition.
