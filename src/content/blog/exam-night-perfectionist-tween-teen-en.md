It's 9:40 on a Sunday night. The unit test is first period tomorrow, and your child is still at the desk, copying the same notes into a third set of colored pens. The practice problems haven't been touched. When you ask how it's going, you get "I'm going to fail," in a voice that cracks halfway through. Their last test came back with a 94, which you know because they spent dinner telling you about the six points they lost.

If you're the parent standing in that doorway, this one is for you. At BloomPath, parents of 10 to 15 year olds tell us this exact evening keeps coming back every exam season. Below: why a kid who is doing fine can still spiral, what to say in the moment, how to tell perfectionism from ordinary nerves, and how to set up a home where a wrong answer costs almost nothing.

## Quick Answer: What Helps a Perfectionist Kid Before a Test?

Lower the stakes of being wrong, and keep the connection. At the moment of panic, skip the pep talk and the "you always do fine." Name the feeling, shrink tonight's job to one small task, and send them to bed on time. Over the longer run, praise the process (the practice, the corrections, the questions asked) instead of the score, and make mistakes visible and ordinary at home. Kids who can be wrong in front of you without losing your warmth are the ones who stop treating every test like a verdict.

## Why Does a Kid Who's Doing Fine Fall Apart Before Exams?

Because the fear is about what a bad score would mean, and the actual grades don't touch it. Somewhere around ages 10 to 12, kids start comparing themselves to everyone in the room. A grade stops being feedback about one chapter and turns into a statement about who they are.

Researchers separate "high standards" from perfectionism. High standards are a goal you can miss and shrug off. Perfectionism carries a rule underneath: if I'm not flawless, people will think less of me. A 2019 meta-analysis by Thomas Curran and Andrew Hill in *Psychological Bulletin*, covering college students from 1989 to 2016, found perfectionism rising over those decades, and the biggest increase was in the kind where you feel others expect you to be perfect. That's the version that makes a kid copy notes at 10 p.m. instead of facing a practice test, because copying can't prove them wrong.

Today's kids also carry the comparison in their pockets. A class group chat lights up with "what did you get?" within minutes of papers coming back. In my view, that group chat does more to feed a perfectionist than anything a teacher says. If your child checks their phone with a knot in their stomach after a test, the pressure is coming from the screen as much as from the grade. ## What Do I Say at 9:40 p.m. When They're Already Crying?

Say less. A child in panic can't take in logic, so the first job is to bring the temperature down. A script that works for many families:

1. **Name it, and don't argue with it.** "You're really scared about tomorrow. That makes sense, you care about doing well." Skip "you'll be fine." They hear that as "you're not listening."
2. **Ask one question.** "What's the part that scares you the most?" Often the answer is small and specific: one chapter, one type of problem. Sometimes it's "I'll let you down." You want to know which.
3. **Shrink the task.** "Let's pick the one thing that would help most in the next 20 minutes." Fifteen practice problems on the shaky topic beat a third copy of notes they already know.
4. **Set the stop time out loud.** "At 10:15 we're done, and sleep is part of studying." Tired brains retrieve worse, and a kid who stays up to 1 a.m. walks into the room more afraid than before.
5. **Say the sentence that matters.** "Whatever the score is tomorrow, dinner is the same, and I'm on your side."

Dr. Becky Kennedy's idea that two things can be true at once fits well here: "I know this test matters to you" and "it's time to stop for tonight." You don't have to pick between warmth and the boundary.

I still get the order wrong sometimes. When I'm tired, my first move is the reassurance speech, and it lands badly every time. Feeling first, plan second works better, even though it feels slower in the moment.

## How Can I Tell Perfectionism From Normal Test Nerves?

Normal nerves show up before the test and fade after it. Perfectionism hangs around. Watch for a pattern over several weeks, not one bad night:

- Spending hours on work that should take minutes, or redoing finished assignments
- Avoiding the thing they fear: delaying the practice test, "forgetting" to start
- Crying or going rigid over a 90-something score
- Hiding grades, or hiding the corrections page, because a mistake feels shameful (we wrote about this in [why kids hide mistakes](/en/blog/child-hiding-mistakes-trust-repair-conversation-en))
- Headaches, stomachaches, or trouble sleeping that cluster around tests
- Giving up on things they aren't instantly good at

A few of these in a stressful week is common. Many of them, for a month or more, or any talk of hurting themselves, is a reason to call your pediatrician or the school counselor. Don't wait for the grades to drop first. Perfectionist kids often keep their grades up right until they can't.

## What Does Montessori Say About Mistakes?

Montessori classrooms are built so a mistake is information the child can find alone. Materials have what Maria Montessori called the "control of error": the child can see for themselves that the cylinder doesn't fit, and fix it without an adult announcing a verdict. The adult doesn't hover with a red pen. The error belongs to the learner, and so does the repair.

You can copy the idea at home, even with a 13-year-old who has never touched a Montessori material:

- **Practice tests are the first draft.** Do them on scrap paper or with a pencil. Mark errors with a plain check or a question mark, no red ink, no sighing.
- **Keep an "error log."** A simple notebook page: the problem, what I did, what I'll do next time. Treat it as a collection of interesting bugs rather than a record of failures. Debugging is normal work, and kids tend to relax when a mistake gets a boring name.
- **Let your child check their own work first.** Hand them the answer key before you look at the paper. The self-check is the skill the test is training.
- **Make your own mistakes audible.** "I double-booked us on Saturday. Here's how I'm fixing it." You don't have to make anything up for effect. Ordinary adult errors happen daily, and narrating one calmly gives your child a model.

This is part of the "prepared environment" idea: you can't remove every pressure from your child's life, but you can arrange the home so that being wrong is cheap there.

## How Should I Talk About Grades When the Test Comes Back?

Ask about the work before you ask about the number. In a well-known set of experiments, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck (1998) gave fifth graders a puzzle and then praised some for being smart and others for working hard. After a harder round, the "smart" group was more likely to pick easy tasks next, and their performance dropped. The "hard work" group chose challenges and kept improving. Praise that points at ability teaches kids that a mistake exposes them. Praise that points at effort and strategy gives them something to do next.

Try these:

- "Which problem was hardest, and what did you try?"
- "What would you do differently in the first ten minutes next time?"
- "I noticed you kept going on that essay even when it was frustrating."

And skip "why did you lose points on that one?" in the first five minutes. If the score is great, ask what they enjoyed. If it's low, "that must sting" goes a long way, and the problem-solving can wait until tomorrow.

## What About the Group Chat and Social Media Comparison?

Agree on a rule before the next test. A calm weekend conversation works better than a blow-up at midnight. One option many families try: phone charges outside the bedroom on exam nights, and your child decides ahead of time whether they'll check scores in the group chat at all. Some kids choose to wait until they've seen the paper themselves. Let them choose. They're more likely to keep a rule they set. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it bad that my child cares so much about grades?**
Caring is fine. It becomes a problem when a grade decides how they feel about themselves for the whole evening, or when the fear makes them avoid studying. The aim is to keep the motivation and loosen the fear.

**Should I tell them grades don't matter?**
They know you're bending the truth, and it can sound like you don't understand. A truer message: "Grades are one piece of information. You matter to me whatever they say."

**Can I help with studying without taking over?**
Yes. Offer to quiz them, or ask them to teach you the topic. Teaching is strong practice and keeps them in charge. Skip rewriting their notes or fixing their answers.

**What if they refuse to talk and just say "I'm fine"?**
Try a side-by-side moment, like a drive or a walk, and keep the question light: "What's the part of this week that's been heaviest?" Then leave silence for them to fill.

**When is test anxiety a sign of something more?**
If it brings physical symptoms most weeks, panic attacks, school avoidance, sleep trouble that lasts, or any talk of self-harm, contact your pediatrician or school counselor promptly.

**Does being a perfectionist mean my child will burn out?**
Not automatically. Perfectionistic kids who feel safe being imperfect at home tend to do better than those who feel every mistake is watched. Your reactions to small errors count for a lot.
