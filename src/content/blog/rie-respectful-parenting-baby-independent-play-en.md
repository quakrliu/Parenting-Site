**TL;DR:** RIE (Resources for Infant Educarers, pronounced "wry") is a caregiving philosophy built by Magda Gerber that treats even very young babies as capable people who can direct their own play — if adults stop narrating, redirecting, and entertaining every second of it. At BloomPath, we get asked constantly whether "just letting the baby play alone" is neglect or genius. The research and Janet Lansbury's own practice both point the same direction: short, safe, uninterrupted play sessions build attention span, problem-solving, and self-trust that constant adult narration actually gets in the way of.

Last October, on a Tuesday afternoon, I was sitting on our living room floor doing what I thought was excellent parenting. My daughter was maybe eight months old, propped on a blanket surrounded by a few wooden rings and a crinkly fabric ball, and I was providing what I can only describe as color commentary. "You picked up the red ring! Can you shake it? Listen to that sound! Now try the ball!" I was narrating her entire existence like a sports broadcaster covering a very low-stakes game.

She wasn't even looking at me. She was looking at the ring, turning it over, mouthing it, dropping it, looking at where it landed. My voice was, if anything, competing with whatever she was actually trying to figure out.

My wife Mei walked in, watched for about ten seconds, and said, "Have you tried just... not saying anything?" I laughed it off. Then I actually tried it the next day, and I lasted maybe ninety seconds before I needed to fill the silence with something. That was the moment I realized how much of my "engagement" with my baby was actually about my own discomfort with quiet, not about her needs.

## What Is RIE Parenting?

RIE stands for Resources for Infant Educarers, a philosophy started in 1978 by early childhood specialist Magda Gerber, building on the work of Hungarian pediatrician Emmi Pikler. The core idea is blunt: babies are whole people from birth, not blank slates waiting for adults to fill them with stimulation. RIE calls its approach "Educaring" — treating basic care routines like diaper changes and feeding as moments of respectful communication, and treating play as something a baby is fully capable of directing themselves, without an adult running the show.

[Janet Lansbury](https://www.janetlansbury.com/), who trained under Gerber and has taught RIE classes in Los Angeles for two decades, has become the most visible modern voice for this approach through her podcast *Unruffled*. In a March 2026 episode, "Why Parents Need RIE," Lansbury and guest Melani Ladygo talked through exactly the pattern I fell into: parents who mistake constant narration and direction for good engagement, when what a baby actually needs most is time and space to work things out on their own terms.

## Is Sitting Back Really Better Than Playing With My Baby?

Short answer: RIE doesn't say never play with your baby — it says protect specific stretches of time where you don't. Lansbury has written that independent play sessions can run anywhere from 15 minutes up to several hours depending on the baby's age and mood, and that the parent's job during that window is to observe, not direct.

Think of it like debugging someone else's code by rewriting it line by line instead of watching where it actually breaks. If I jump in and hand my daughter the toy she's struggling to open, or narrate what she "should" try next, I've just solved the problem for her. She never gets the data on what worked and what didn't. RIE's bet is that a baby left with safe, appropriately simple materials and zero narration will build focus and frustration tolerance that a constantly-cued baby doesn't get the chance to build.

## What Does RIE Say About Independent Play, Specifically?

The RIE environment for independent play is deliberately boring by adult standards: a few open-ended objects (a wooden ring, a scarf, a ball, a set of stacking cups), a flat safe surface, and nothing that requires a battery or a screen to be interesting. Gerber's own writing describes wanting an environment "safe enough that a baby could be left completely alone in it" — not because you're leaving, but because the safety bar removes your need to hover and intervene.

Lansbury's practical guidance, based on 20+ years of RIE classes, breaks down into a few repeatable moves:

1. **Pick a boring-on-purpose toy set.** Two or three open-ended objects beat a basket of flashy, single-purpose toys. Fewer choices, more focus.
2. **Get low, then get quiet.** Sit near your baby at their level so you're available, but resist narrating what they're doing.
3. **Let struggle happen before you help.** A baby grunting at a stuck ring is doing cognitive work. Step in only if they signal real distress, not mild effort.
4. **Start small and build the muscle — yours, not just theirs.** Five quiet minutes is a realistic starting point for a parent who's used to narrating everything. It was for me.
5. **Treat caregiving moments (diaper changes, feeding) as the connection time**, so independent play doesn't have to double as bonding time too.

## How Is RIE Different From Montessori?

RIE and Montessori overlap heavily — both trust the child's competence, both emphasize a prepared, safe environment, and both ask adults to observe more than direct. The difference is mostly structural: Montessori is a full educational method with certified guides, specific developmental materials, and a scope that runs from birth through adolescence. RIE was built specifically for infants and very young toddlers (roughly birth to age two) and lives mostly in the home, not the classroom, with no branded materials of its own.

In practice, a lot of families — us included — end up doing RIE-style independent play during infancy and then sliding into a more Montessori-influenced home setup once our daughter hit toddlerhood and started wanting actual "work" to do. ## What If My Baby Cries or Won't Play Alone at First?

This is the part nobody tells you: it's often worse before it's better, especially if your baby is used to constant adult input. If you've been narrating and redirecting for months, your baby has learned that play means you're involved. Pulling back cold turkey can trigger protest — not because independent play is wrong for them, but because you're changing a pattern they've come to expect.

Lansbury's advice here (and what actually worked for us) is to shrink the ask. Instead of aiming for 30 minutes of solo play, aim for three quiet minutes while you sit nearby doing something else — folding laundry, answering a text, anything that visibly signals you're present but not performing. Build from there. If your baby is genuinely distressed, not just testing the new quiet, that's a cue to pick them up — RIE isn't about ignoring real needs, it's about not treating every small struggle as one.

## Does Research Actually Support This?

The specific RIE brand hasn't been the subject of large randomized studies the way Montessori has (see our breakdown of the [2025 national Montessori study](/en/blog/montessori-worth-it-uva-study-2025-en) if you want that side of the research). But the underlying developmental claims — that unstructured, self-directed play builds executive function, sustained attention, and problem-solving more effectively than adult-directed play — are well established in developmental psychology, going back to research on child-led play and autonomy support. Emmi Pikler's original longitudinal observations at the Loczy Institute in Budapest, tracking infants raised with minimal adult interference during play, found those children reached motor milestones on typical or ahead-of-typical timelines while showing strong independent problem-solving.

What I can tell you from the parent side, not the research side, is narrower but real: once I actually gave my daughter that quiet floor time consistently, her stretches of focused, self-directed play got noticeably longer over a few weeks. That's an anecdote, not a study — but it's the same pattern Lansbury describes hearing from parents in her classes for two decades.

## How Do I Know If My Home Setup Is RIE-Friendly?

Walk through your baby's usual play spot and ask what would happen if you left the room entirely for two minutes. If the honest answer involves anything that could tip over, choke, or requires a plug, it's not there yet. RIE-friendly setups tend to be low, flat, and boring: a play mat or rug, a few objects at floor level, nothing mounted above the baby's head, no swings or bouncers doing the entertaining for you. ## FAQ

**What does RIE stand for in parenting?**
RIE stands for Resources for Infant Educarers, a respectful caregiving philosophy developed by Magda Gerber in 1978, based on the work of pediatrician Emmi Pikler. It emphasizes treating babies as capable individuals and giving them uninterrupted time for self-directed play.

**Is RIE parenting the same as Montessori?**
No, though they overlap. RIE is specifically designed for infants and toddlers up to around age two and has no branded materials or classroom structure. Montessori is a full educational method spanning birth through adolescence, with certified guides and specific developmental materials. Both emphasize observing over directing.

**How long should independent play sessions be for a baby?**
Janet Lansbury's guidance suggests sessions can range from as little as 15 minutes up to several hours depending on the baby's age, mood, and how accustomed they are to solo play. If your baby is new to it, start with just a few minutes and build up gradually.

**What age is RIE parenting designed for?**
RIE was designed primarily for infants and toddlers from birth to roughly age two, though many of its principles about respectful communication and observation carry forward well into the toddler and preschool years.

**Is RIE parenting supported by research?**
The RIE brand itself hasn't been studied in large controlled trials, but the underlying idea — that self-directed, unstructured play builds attention and problem-solving skills — is well supported in developmental psychology, including Emmi Pikler's decades of longitudinal observation at the Loczy Institute in Budapest.

**What do I do if my baby cries during independent play?**
Distinguish between mild frustration (a grunt at a stuck toy, which is productive struggle) and real distress (sustained crying, seeking you out). RIE says respond to real distress — it isn't about ignoring your baby, it's about not rescuing them from every small challenge.
