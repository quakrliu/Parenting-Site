One January, about eight months into parenthood, I was standing in our kitchen at 2 AM making a bottle for the fourth night in a row. Mei was asleep — or trying to be — and I remember staring at the microwave clock thinking: *we are never going to sleep through the night again.* That was the moment I started actually researching night weaning instead of just white-knuckling through it, and it's the reason BloomPath's sleep content exists at all — we build the resource we wished we'd had at 2 AM.

**TL;DR**: Most babies can start dropping night feeds somewhere between 4 and 9 months, once they're eating solids and gaining weight steadily — but check with your pediatrician first, since every baby's growth curve is different. The gentle approach isn't "just stop feeding at night." It's shifting calories to daytime, stretching the gap between night feeds by small increments, and eventually swapping milk for water or comfort. For us, it took about 12 days, with one rough night in the middle that almost made us quit.

## What Does "Night Weaning" Actually Mean?

Night weaning means gradually stopping milk feeds between roughly 11 PM and 6 AM — not weaning off breastfeeding or bottles entirely, and not the same thing as sleep training. Your baby can still nurse or take a bottle during the day; you're just working toward a stretch of nighttime hours without a feed.

This trips a lot of parents up, including me. I thought "night weaning" meant we were done with bottles altogether. Mei had to explain — she'd read three different sleep books by that point — that we were just moving the calories to daylight hours, not cutting them.

## When Should You Start Night Weaning?

Most babies are developmentally ready somewhere between 4 and 9 months, once they're eating solids consistently and their pediatrician confirms their weight gain is on track. There's no single magic week. A baby who's still under 12-13 pounds, was born early, or has reflux or a medical condition may genuinely need that middle-of-the-night feed longer than a bigger, older baby does.

Here's the two-question check we used, and one I'd suggest running by your own pediatrician before you start:

1. Is my baby eating three solid meals a day and gaining weight on schedule?
2. Has my pediatrician confirmed there's no medical reason (reflux, low weight, prematurity) to keep the night feed?

If both answers are yes, you're probably in the window. If you're not sure, that 15-minute check-in with your pediatrician is worth more than any blog post — including this one.

## How Do You Night Wean Gently?

The gentle method is three moves: load up daytime calories, stretch the gap between night feeds gradually, and eventually replace milk with something else at the last waking. Here's exactly what we did, in order.

**1. Front-load the day.** For about a week before we touched the nighttime schedule, we made sure our daughter was getting full, unhurried feeds every 2.5 to 3.5 hours during the day, plus solids at three meals. A baby who's genuinely full during the day has less physical need to make it up at night.

**2. Stretch, don't cut.** Instead of skipping a night feed cold turkey, we pushed the first waking back by 10 to 15 minutes each night — soothing with rocking or a pat on the back instead of immediately feeding. Night one, we made her wait 10 extra minutes before the bottle. By night five, we were pushing an hour.

**3. Swap the association.** Around month 9, once she was drinking from a cup during the day, we started offering a small cup of water instead of milk at the final night waking. This is the step that actually broke the "wake up = milk" pattern for us — she'd take a few sips of water, realize it wasn't the reward she was expecting, and settle back down faster than we thought possible.

**4. Get everyone on the same page.** My mother-in-law watched her one night during week two and, without telling us, gave her a full bottle at 3 AM because she "seemed hungry." We were back to square one for two nights. If grandparents, babysitters, or a night-shift partner are in the rotation, the whole household needs the same plan — not just the person reading this article.

## How Long Does Night Weaning Actually Take?

For us, it took about 12 days from the first stretched feed to a full night without one — but night 4 was genuinely worse than night 1, which nearly made us give up. That temporary regression is common; some sleep researchers describe a similar pattern in sleep training as an "extinction burst," where a behavior spikes right before it drops off. We wrote more about that pattern in [our piece on the night gentle sleep training nearly broke us](/en/blog/gentle-sleep-training-extinction-burst-en). Knowing that spike was coming — and probably temporary — is the only reason we didn't cave and go back to full night feeds.

Most sources put the realistic range at one to three weeks, depending on your baby's age, temperament, and how consistent the household is. If you're past three weeks with zero progress, that's a good signal to loop back to your pediatrician rather than keep pushing alone.

## What If My Baby Cries or Fights It?

Some fussing at the stretched-out feed times is normal and doesn't mean you're doing it wrong — it means your baby is protesting a routine change, the same way they'd protest any change to something predictable. What's not normal is a baby who seems genuinely distressed, isn't gaining weight, or is waking dramatically more than before — that's your cue to pause and call your pediatrician, not push through.

We leaned on picking her up, patting, and quiet shushing instead of the bottle during the stretched window. It was slower than just feeding her back to sleep, and some nights I was too tired to care about the "right" way to do it. That's normal too. You don't have to execute this perfectly for it to work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is night weaning the same as sleep training?**
No. Night weaning is specifically about stopping night feeds; sleep training is a broader approach to helping a baby fall asleep independently. You can night wean without doing any formal sleep training, and vice versa.

**Will night weaning hurt my milk supply if I'm breastfeeding?**
For most parents past the newborn stage, dropping one or two night feeds gradually doesn't meaningfully affect supply, especially if you're maintaining daytime nursing sessions. If you're exclusively breastfeeding and worried about supply, this is worth a specific conversation with a lactation consultant before you start.

**My baby is 5 months old and still waking twice a night. Is something wrong?**
Not necessarily. Some babies genuinely need more calories overnight at 5 months than others, especially smaller or premature babies. Two night wakings at 5 months isn't automatically a red flag — but it's a reasonable thing to bring up at your next pediatrician visit.

**What if my baby was sleeping through the night and then started waking again?**
That's usually a regression tied to a growth spurt, teething, or a developmental leap — not a sign that night weaning "failed." We wrote about the most common causes in [our guide to toddlers waking every two hours](/en/blog/toddler-wakes-every-2-hours-en).

**Can grandparents or a babysitter mess up night weaning progress?**
Yes, easily — as we found out the hard way. One inconsistent night with a different caregiver can reset several nights of progress. Write down the plan and make sure everyone in the rotation, including grandparents, has seen it.
