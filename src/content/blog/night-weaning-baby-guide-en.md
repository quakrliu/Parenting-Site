If you've ever stood in the kitchen at 2 AM making a bottle for the fourth night in a row, staring at the microwave clock and thinking *we are never going to sleep through the night again* — this one's for you. Instead of white-knuckling through it, it helps to actually understand how night weaning works. That's what BloomPath's sleep content is for: the resource parents wish they had at 2 AM.

**TL;DR**: Most babies can start dropping night feeds somewhere between 4 and 9 months, once they're eating solids and gaining weight steadily — but check with your pediatrician first, since every baby's growth curve is different. The gentle approach isn't "just stop feeding at night." It's shifting calories to daytime, stretching the gap between night feeds by small increments, and eventually swapping milk for water or comfort. Expect it to take one to three weeks, often with a rough night in the middle that makes you want to quit.

## What Does "Night Weaning" Actually Mean?

Night weaning means gradually stopping milk feeds between roughly 11 PM and 6 AM — not weaning off breastfeeding or bottles entirely, and not the same thing as sleep training. Your baby can still nurse or take a bottle during the day; you're just working toward a stretch of nighttime hours without a feed.

This trips a lot of parents up. It's easy to assume "night weaning" means you're done with bottles altogether. You're not — you're just moving the calories to daylight hours, not cutting them.

## When Should You Start Night Weaning?

Most babies are developmentally ready somewhere between 4 and 9 months, once they're eating solids consistently and their pediatrician confirms their weight gain is on track. There's no single magic week. A baby who's still under 12-13 pounds, was born early, or has reflux or a medical condition may genuinely need that middle-of-the-night feed longer than a bigger, older baby does.

Here's a two-question check worth running by your own pediatrician before you start:

1. Is my baby eating three solid meals a day and gaining weight on schedule?
2. Has my pediatrician confirmed there's no medical reason (reflux, low weight, prematurity) to keep the night feed?

If both answers are yes, you're probably in the window. If you're not sure, that 15-minute check-in with your pediatrician is worth more than any blog post — including this one.

## How Do You Night Wean Gently?

The gentle method is three moves: load up daytime calories, stretch the gap between night feeds gradually, and eventually replace milk with something else at the last waking. Here's how that looks, in order.

**1. Front-load the day.** For about a week before you touch the nighttime schedule, make sure your baby is getting full, unhurried feeds during the day, plus solids at three meals. A baby who's genuinely full during the day has less physical need to make it up at night.

**2. Stretch, don't cut.** Instead of skipping a night feed cold turkey, push the feed at the first waking back by 10 to 15 minutes each night — soothing with rocking or a pat on the back instead of immediately feeding. Over several nights, those small increments add up.

**3. Swap the association.** Once your baby is drinking from a cup during the day, try offering a small cup of water instead of milk at the final night waking. This step is often what breaks the "wake up = milk" pattern — a few sips of water aren't the reward the baby was expecting, and that alone can help them settle back down.

**4. Get everyone on the same page.** One well-meaning caregiver giving a full bottle at 3 AM because the baby "seemed hungry" can put you back at square one. If grandparents, babysitters, or a night-shift partner are in the rotation, the whole household needs the same plan — not just the person reading this article.

## How Long Does Night Weaning Actually Take?

Expect some nights to get worse before they get better — a night partway through can be rougher than the first one, and that's usually when parents want to give up. That temporary regression is common; some sleep researchers describe a similar pattern in sleep training as an "extinction burst," where a behavior spikes right before it drops off. We wrote more about that pattern in [our piece on the extinction burst in gentle sleep training](/en/blog/gentle-sleep-training-extinction-burst-en). Knowing that spike may be coming — and is probably temporary — makes it easier not to cave and go back to full night feeds.

Most sources put the realistic range at one to three weeks, depending on your baby's age, temperament, and how consistent the household is. If you're past three weeks with zero progress, that's a good signal to loop back to your pediatrician rather than keep pushing alone.

## What If My Baby Cries or Fights It?

Some fussing at the stretched-out feed times is normal and doesn't mean you're doing it wrong — it means your baby is protesting a routine change, the same way they'd protest any change to something predictable. What's not normal is a baby who seems genuinely distressed, isn't gaining weight, or is waking dramatically more than before — that's your cue to pause and call your pediatrician, not push through.

During the stretched window, picking your baby up, patting, and quiet shushing can stand in for the bottle. It's slower than just feeding back to sleep, and some nights you'll be too tired to care about the "right" way to do it. That's normal too. You don't have to execute this perfectly for it to work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is night weaning the same as sleep training?**
No. Night weaning is specifically about stopping night feeds; sleep training is a broader approach to helping a baby fall asleep independently. You can night wean without doing any formal sleep training, and vice versa.

**Will night weaning hurt my milk supply if I'm breastfeeding?**
For most parents past the newborn stage, dropping one or two night feeds gradually doesn't meaningfully affect supply, especially if you're maintaining daytime nursing sessions. If you're exclusively breastfeeding and worried about supply, this is worth a specific conversation with a lactation consultant before you start.

**My baby is 5 months old and still waking twice a night. Is something wrong?**
Not necessarily. Some babies genuinely need more calories overnight at 5 months than others, especially smaller or premature babies. Two night wakings at 5 months isn't automatically a red flag — but it's a reasonable thing to bring up at your next pediatrician visit.

**What if my baby was sleeping through the night and then started waking again?**
That's usually a regression tied to a growth spurt, teething, or a developmental leap — not a sign that night weaning "failed." We wrote about the most common causes in [our guide to toddlers waking every two hours](/en/blog/toddler-wakes-every-2-hours-en).

**Can grandparents or a babysitter mess up night weaning progress?**
Yes, easily. One inconsistent night with a different caregiver can reset several nights of progress. Write down the plan and make sure everyone in the rotation, including grandparents, has seen it.
