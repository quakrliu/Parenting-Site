**TL;DR:** Splitting chores 50/50 doesn't mean splitting the *mental load* 50/50. The invisible work — remembering, anticipating, planning, tracking — usually still lands on one parent. Sociologist Allison Daminger's research names this pattern precisely. BloomPath's Ethan Moore breaks down what changed once he and his wife stopped counting chores and started assigning full ownership instead.

Last Tuesday at 11:40 PM, I was lying in bed, feeling pretty good about myself. I'd unloaded the dishwasher, packed our daughter's water bottle for the morning, and hauled the recycling out to the curb. Three tasks. Nobody reminded me. I felt like husband of the year.

Mei was still in the kitchen, thumbs moving on her phone. I asked what she was doing.

"Booking Luna's dentist follow-up," she said, not looking up. "Trying to find a slot that doesn't overlap with her teacher conference, which I also need to reschedule because it's the same week as her friend's birthday party, which I still haven't RSVP'd to."

I had done three things. She was running an entire operating system.

That gap — between "I helped" and "I own it" — is what researchers call the mental load, and it turns out there's actual data on why it exists, why it's so hard to see, and what fixes it. Not vibes. Data.

## What Is the Mental Load, Exactly?

The mental load is the cognitive work of running a household — the remembering, planning, and tracking that happens before any visible task gets done.

Sociologist Allison Daminger spent months interviewing 35 couples for a 2019 study published in *American Sociological Review*, and she found that household labor actually has four distinct stages: anticipating a need, identifying options to meet it, deciding between them, and monitoring the outcome. Physical execution — the part everyone notices — is only one piece of that chain, and often the smallest one.

Here's the part that stung when I read it: Daminger found that in couples with young kids, women disproportionately carried the *anticipating* and *monitoring* stages — the invisible bookends. Men were more likely to be credited with "deciding," the visible, high-status middle step. So a dad picking the pediatrician from a shortlist his wife already researched, called, and vetted feels like teamwork. It's actually a fraction of the job.

I'm an engineer. Give me this in system terms and it clicks immediately: I was the guy who ran the deploy script when someone else already wrote the code, tested it, found the bugs, and told me exactly when to push the button. That's not nothing. But it's also not "we built this together."

## Why Does This Happen Even When Chores Are Split Evenly?

It happens because chores are visible and cognitive labor isn't, so couples default to counting the thing they can see.

Before that Tuesday night, if you'd asked me to rate our division of labor, I would've said 60/40, maybe 55/45 in Mei's favor on a bad week. I was counting diaper changes, school drop-offs, dishes. What I wasn't counting — because I genuinely couldn't see it — was who noticed we were low on the specific allergy-friendly snack Luna's school required, who remembered the library books were due, who kept a running mental list of which of Luna's shoes didn't fit anymore.

A friend from my old team, Priya, told me something similar happened at her house — except in reverse, with her husband as the default rememberer for anything tech- or finance-related. The mental load isn't inherently gendered; it's whoever's brain becomes the household's junk drawer, and in most heterosexual couples with young kids, the research shows that's still disproportionately the mom.

None of this means the other partner is lazy. I wasn't lazy. I was doing real work. I just wasn't doing the *first* work — the noticing. ## How Do You Know If You're Carrying the Mental Load?

You're carrying it if you're the one who knows things without ever being told to know them.

A quick, honest checklist — I failed most of these before that Tuesday:

- Do you know your kid's current shoe size without checking a box?
- Do you know the date of the next pediatrician appointment right now, off the top of your head?
- Do you notice a permission slip deadline before your partner mentions it?
- When you go on a trip, do you pack for the kids, or does your partner hand you a list?
- If your partner disappeared for a week, would the household routines survive without a document explaining them?

If you answered "my partner" to most of these, you're not the one carrying it. If you answered "me," you probably are — and you likely didn't apply for the job. Nobody does. It just accumulates.

## What Actually Fixed It in Our House?

What worked wasn't dividing tasks more evenly. It was assigning full ownership of entire tasks, start to finish.

Mei found Eve Rodsky's *Fair Play* system a few months after that Tuesday-night wake-up call, and the core idea reframed everything for us: a task isn't really shared just because two people touch different parts of it. Rodsky's framework has each partner take full "cards" — conception, planning, *and* execution — for specific domains. That's the shift: instead of "I'll do the dishes if you remind me," full ownership means I decide when the dishwasher runs, I notice when we're low on detergent, and I buy more before we run out. Nobody has to ask.

We picked our cards over a Sunday morning coffee, which sounds simple and was still slightly humbling. I took: school forms, Luna's shoes and clothes sizing, meal planning three nights a week, and the recycling/trash system end to end — not just carrying bins to the curb, but knowing pickup days and what counts as recyclable in our city. Mei kept: medical appointments, teacher communication, and the family calendar backbone.

The test Rodsky uses, which I now repeat to any dad who'll listen: if you own a card, your partner should never have to think about it. Not "remind me" — never think about it. That's the actual bar for full ownership, and it's a much higher bar than "I helped."

## What Do You Actually Say to Start This Conversation?

You start with a specific, recent example — not a general accusation.

"You never remember anything" starts a fight. "Last week I was the one who booked the dentist, the haircut, and RSVP'd to the birthday party — can we sit down and split who owns tracking that stuff going forward?" starts a conversation, because it's a system problem, not a character flaw.

A few scripts that worked for us, and might work for you:

- "I want to try something — can we each pick five things we'll own completely, so the other person literally doesn't have to think about them?"
- "I think I've been doing tasks, but you've been running the schedule. Can we talk about what that's actually costing you?"
- "What's one thing that's currently living only in your head that you'd want out of it?"

That last one is the one that got Mei talking for twenty straight minutes, and honestly, it should have.

## Is This Just a "Women's Issue"?

No — it's a household-systems issue that happens to fall along gender lines most often, which is exactly why it's worth naming out loud instead of assuming it'll balance itself out.

I'm not going to pretend the research is neutral on this. Daminger's data, and a separate 2019 study by Ciciolla and Luthar in *Sex Roles*, both found that mothers of young kids carry disproportionately more invisible household labor even when both partners work full time — and that this imbalance is linked to higher maternal exhaustion and lower relationship satisfaction. That's not a feeling. That's measured. It also tracks with what we've seen in readers dealing with [parental burnout](/en/blog/summer-activity-planning-burnout-balance-en) more broadly — exhaustion rarely comes from one big thing, it comes from a hundred untracked small things.

But the fix isn't guilt. It's structure. Once Mei and I stopped treating "help" as the goal and started treating "full ownership of specific things" as the goal, the resentment that had been quietly building for years — the kind neither of us wanted to name — actually had somewhere to go besides in circles.

## The Uncomfortable Confession

Here's mine: for years, I genuinely believed I was an equal partner because I never said no when Mei asked me to do something. I thought "always available when asked" was the same as "sharing the load." It's not. Being asked means someone else already did the noticing, the planning, and the deciding about whether to ask you in the first place. Being asked is the very last, smallest step in a long invisible chain.

I still get parts of this wrong. Last month I forgot it was picture day at school — a card that's technically mine now — because I hadn't checked the shared calendar in four days. Mei didn't fix it for me. She let me scramble, apologize to Luna's teacher, and feel exactly the low-grade panic she used to feel every single week before we changed anything. That was fair. It was also the best lesson I've gotten out of this entire process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the mental load in parenting?**
The mental load is the invisible cognitive work of running a household and raising kids — remembering, planning, anticipating, and tracking everything before any physical task happens. Daminger's 2019 research broke it into four stages: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, and monitoring outcomes.

**Why does the mental load fall on one parent even when chores are split evenly?**
Because chores are visible and cognitive labor isn't. Research shows one partner — usually the mother in heterosexual couples with young kids — disproportionately handles the anticipating and monitoring stages, the invisible parts before and after a visible task.

**How do you know if you're carrying the mental load?**
If you're the one who knows your kid's shoe size, the next appointment date, and upcoming deadlines without ever being told to track them, you're likely carrying it.

**What is the Fair Play method?**
Fair Play, from Eve Rodsky, assigns each task as a full "card" one partner owns entirely — conception, planning, and execution — instead of splitting visible steps between two people.

**How do you bring this up without starting a fight?**
Lead with a specific recent example instead of a general accusation, and frame it as a system problem you're solving together, not a character flaw.
