It's Sunday night and you're at the kitchen table with the laptop open, adding up December. Your 10-year-old wanders in for a snack, glances at the screen, and then stops. "Half my class is going skiing over winter break. Why don't we ever go anywhere?" A pause. Then, quieter: "Are we poor?"

You feel your face get warm. Maybe money really is tight this year. Maybe it isn't, and you just don't want to spend $3,000 on a ski trip. Either way, the question lands somewhere tender. BloomPath put this guide together for parents of 9 to 12 year olds who are starting to get these questions and want to answer them honestly, without lecturing and without handing their own money worries to their child.

## TL;DR: How Should I Answer "Are We Poor?"

Pause before you answer, and ask what made them wonder. Most of the time, "Are we poor?" is really "Am I different from my friends?" or "Are we going to be okay?" Answer that underlying question first, with something calm and true: "We have what we need, and we make choices about what we spend on." Then, over the following weeks, give your child a small, real role in family spending decisions. Kids this age handle honest, age-sized information much better than silence or vague reassurance.

## Why Is My Tween Suddenly Asking About Money?

Because between about 9 and 12, children start comparing themselves to peers in a much more detailed way, and money is one of the most visible differences. They notice whose family flies somewhere over break, who has the new phone, which kids get takeout every Friday. Before this age, many kids accept "that's what our family does" without much thought. Now they're building a picture of where they fit.

Jane Nelsen's *Positive Discipline*, which draws on Alfred Adler's psychology, frames most children's behavior around two needs: belonging and significance. A money question at this age usually carries the belonging part. Most of the time, your child is quietly trying to work out whether being different from their friends means something is wrong.

When our daughter was younger, I'd have heard a question like this as a complaint and gotten a little defensive. Over the years I've come to hear it as a sign of trust. Your child brought a worry to you instead of carrying it around alone, and that's worth protecting.

## What Should I Say in the Moment?

Ask a question back, name the feeling, and give one short, steady answer. You don't need to have the whole money conversation at 9 p.m. on a Sunday.

1. **Get curious first.** "What made you think about that?" Ron Lieber, the *New York Times* money columnist and author of *The Opposite of Spoiled*, recommends this move when kids ask money questions, because the real question is often different from the one they asked. Maybe a friend made a comment. Maybe your child overheard you on the phone.
2. **Name what you hear.** "Sounds like it felt weird when everyone was talking about their ski trips and you didn't have one to share." Daniel Siegel calls this "name it to tame it": putting words to a feeling helps a child's thinking brain come back online.
3. **Give a calm, true answer.** "We're not poor. We have a home, food, and what we need. Families make different choices about what to spend on, and a ski trip isn't one of ours this year."
4. **Leave the door open.** "That's a good question, and you can always ask me about money stuff. Want to talk more about it this weekend?"

If money really is tight right now, you don't have to pretend it isn't. You can say, "Things are a bit tighter this year, so we're being careful. The grown-ups are handling it, and you're not going to go without what you need." That last sentence matters most. Kids can handle "we're being careful." What frightens them is a feeling that something big is wrong and nobody will tell them.

## What Should I Avoid Saying?

A few common responses tend to backfire with tweens:

- **"That's none of your business."** It teaches your child that money is a scary secret, and they'll go looking for answers from friends or the internet.
- **"Do you know how lucky you are?"** It may be true, but it turns their honest question into a moral failing. Gratitude grows more from involvement than from guilt.
- **"We can't afford it"** as an automatic answer to everything. If it isn't strictly true, kids figure that out. If it is, repeating it every day can make them anxious. "That's not something we're choosing to spend on" is usually more accurate.
- **Detailed money stress.** Venting about bills, debt, or arguments about spending is adult business. Your child needs to know the grown-ups are steering the ship, even when the water is rough.

## How Much Should I Tell a 9 to 12 Year Old About Our Finances?

Enough to make the world make sense, and not so much that they feel responsible for it. A useful test: will this information help my child understand our choices, or will it give them something to worry about that they can't change?

Things most tweens can handle:

- Roughly how a family budget works: money comes in, it gets split between needs, wants, saving, and giving.
- Real prices of everyday things. Kids this age are often shocked by what groceries, rent, or a car repair cost, and that surprise is useful.
- Why your family prioritizes what it does. "We'd rather spend on visiting Grandma every summer than on a ski trip" is a values statement, and kids remember those.
- That different families have different incomes and that it isn't a measure of how good a person is.

Things that are usually better kept with the adults: exact debt amounts, worries about job loss before anything has happened, and tension between parents about money.

## What Does Montessori Say About Kids and Money?

Montessori doesn't have a money curriculum for families, but its core idea applies directly: children learn by doing real work in a real environment, with responsibility that grows as they show they can handle it. Montessori called this *freedom within limits*.

Montessori elementary programs have a tradition called "Going Out," where small groups of older children plan trips outside the classroom themselves, figuring out logistics like transportation and cost with adult support. The spirit is the same one you can bring home: let children handle actual decisions, inside boundaries you've set.

Here's what that can look like for a 9 to 12 year old:

- **Give them a real slice of the grocery budget.** Hand your child $20 and the job of buying snacks or ingredients for one family dinner. They'll compare prices without you saying a word.
- **Plan one outing together with a fixed budget.** "We have $60 for a fun Saturday. What can we do?" Suddenly the ski trip question turns into a creative problem your child gets to solve.
- **Let them manage a small allowance with their own choices.** Some families split it into save, spend, and give. If you're wondering whether to tie it to chores, our guide on [whether kids should get paid for chores](/en/blog/should-kids-be-paid-for-chores-montessori-en) walks through the Montessori view.
- **Show the trade-offs out loud.** When you skip something, say why: "I'd love that jacket, but I'm saving for our trip in the spring." Children learn far more from watching you choose than from being told money doesn't grow on trees.

Over years of parenting, what we figured out is that kids who feel involved worry less. When a child understands how choices get made, "we're not doing that" stops sounding like a mysterious door slamming shut.

## What If My Child Feels Left Out Because of Money?

Take the social feeling seriously, then help them find ways to belong that don't depend on spending. Being the one kid in the group who didn't go somewhere or doesn't own something can sting at this age, and telling them it doesn't matter won't make it stop stinging.

Some things that help:

- **Separate the feeling from the purchase.** "It's hard to sit there while everyone talks about their trip. You're allowed to feel jealous. Feeling that doesn't make you a bad friend."
- **Help them prepare a line.** Tweens often feel better with something ready to say: "We're doing a staycation. I'm going to try that new climbing gym." It gives them a way into the conversation.
- **Look for low-cost connection.** Offer to host a movie night, a baking afternoon, or a park meetup. Belonging comes mostly from time together.
- **Watch for bigger patterns.** If your child is being teased about money or clothes, treat it like any other unkindness at school. Our guide on [what to say when your child compares themselves to a classmate](/en/blog/kid-compares-self-to-classmate-ages-6-9-en) has more on helping kids with that sinking "everyone else has more" feeling.

## What If I'm the One Who Feels Ashamed?

That's very common, and it's worth noticing before the next conversation. Money carries a lot of history. Many of us grew up in homes where it was either never discussed or was the thing parents fought about, and a child's innocent question can bring all of that back in a second.

I've gotten this wrong more times than I'd like. A question about money would hit a nerve, and my answer would come out sharper or more anxious than I meant. What helped was simply giving myself a beat: "That's a really good question. Let me think about it and we'll talk tomorrow." It's completely fine to come back later. Dr. Becky Kennedy writes in *Good Inside* about repair: going back to your child after a conversation that went sideways and taking responsibility for your part. A repaired conversation often teaches more than a perfect one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I tell my 10-year-old how much money we make?**
There's no single right answer. Many money educators suggest that exact income figures aren't necessary at this age, but a general sense of how your budget works is. If your child asks directly, you can ask what they'd do with the number and decide from there.

**How do I explain why other families have more money?**
Keep it simple and non-judgmental: families have different jobs, different incomes, and different priorities. Make clear that how much money a family has says nothing about how good or kind they are.

**Is it okay to say "we can't afford it"?**
Use it when it's true. When it isn't, "that's not something we're choosing to spend on" is more honest and teaches your child that spending is a series of choices.

**My child seems anxious about money. What should I do?**
Reassure them that the adults are handling things and that they will have what they need. Reduce how often adult money stress happens within earshot. If the worry is persistent or affecting sleep or school, talk to your pediatrician or a counselor.

**What age should kids start getting an allowance?**
Many families start somewhere in the early elementary years. By 9 to 12, an allowance with some real choices attached is a practical way to learn budgeting, saving, and trade-offs.

**What does Montessori say about teaching kids about money?**
Montessori focuses on real-life responsibility and freedom within limits. For money, that means giving kids actual budgets and decisions to manage, sized to what they can handle, with adults setting the boundaries.
