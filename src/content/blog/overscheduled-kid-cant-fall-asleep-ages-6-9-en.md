It's 9:52 on a Tuesday. You've finally sat down. The dishwasher is humming, and the spelling list is signed. Then a small voice comes down the hallway: "I can't fall asleep. My brain won't turn off."

You do the math in your head. School until 3. After-care until 4:30. Swim at 5. Dinner eaten in the back seat. Math worksheet at 7:45, a shower, two pages of a book, lights out at 9. Every block of that day made sense when you signed up for it. Together, they left your child lying in the dark with a body that's tired and a mind that's still sprinting.

If that hallway voice is familiar, this BloomPath guide is for you. It's written for parents of 6- to 9-year-olds whose weeknights have quietly filled up, and whose kids can't wind down at bedtime. You'll get what to say tonight, why a full schedule keeps young kids awake, and how to rebuild your evenings without quitting everything.

## Quick Answer

A child who goes straight from activity to homework to bed often has no time to downshift, so the mind keeps running after the lights go off. Tonight, keep your voice low, skip the lecture, and offer a short, boring wind-down. Over the next few weeks, protect at least one weeknight with no scheduled activity, build a predictable 30- to 45-minute screen-free wind-down, and invite your child to help decide which activities stay. Kids aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night, so a 10:30 p.m. fall-asleep time on a school night usually means the schedule needs adjusting.

## Why can't my child fall asleep after a busy day?

Because being physically tired and being ready to sleep are two different states, and a packed evening gives the first without the second. Sleep needs a runway: a stretch of dim, quiet, predictable time when the body's alertness drops. When the last scheduled thing ends at 8:45 and lights go out at 9, there's no runway at all.

I like to picture it as a bike ride downhill with no brakes. Your child has been pedaling hard since 7 a.m. When you say "time for bed," you're asking them to stop on the spot. Most 7-year-olds can't do that. Their ability to calm their own body and thoughts is still developing, which is why they need adults to slow the evening down for them.

There's also the matter of when they process the day. Adults often replay their day in the shower or on the drive home. A child whose every minute was scheduled gets their first quiet moment when the light clicks off. That's when the worry about tomorrow's spelling test or the friend who didn't sit with them at lunch finally arrives.

## How much sleep does a 6- to 9-year-old actually need?

Children aged 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep per night, according to a consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine that the American Academy of Pediatrics has endorsed. For a 7-year-old who wakes at 6:45 for school, that means asleep somewhere between 7:45 and 9:45 p.m.

Run your own numbers for a second. If your child is regularly drifting off at 10:15 and getting up at 6:45, that's 8.5 hours, below the recommended range. Kids rarely announce that they're short on sleep. It tends to show up as tears over a missing sock, a meltdown at pickup, or a teacher's note that they "seem distracted after lunch."

## What should I say tonight when they call out "I can't sleep"?

Say something short and calm that tells your child their body knows how to do this, then stay boring. Any big conversation at 10 p.m. wakes the brain back up.

Try one of these from the doorway:

- "Your body had a really full day. It's still slowing down. That's okay."
- "You don't have to fall asleep. You just have to rest. Sleep will find you."
- "I'll sit here for five minutes. No talking, just breathing."

Then do one quiet thing together. Lay a hand on their back and breathe slowly enough that they can hear it. Or try the "five things" game in a whisper: five things you can hear, four you can feel. If a worry tumbles out, write it on a sticky note and put it on their door. "It's on the paper now. We'll look at it after breakfast." A small 2018 study from Baylor University's sleep lab found that young adults who spent five minutes writing a to-do list for the next day fell asleep faster than those who wrote about what they'd already done. Kids aren't young adults, but I find the sticky note works on the same idea: the thought gets somewhere to go.

What to skip tonight: "You have to sleep NOW or you'll be exhausted tomorrow." Your child already feels that pressure. Adding a deadline to falling asleep usually makes it take longer.

## Is my child over-scheduled?

Your child is probably over-scheduled if most weeknights have a fixed activity, dinner regularly happens in transit, and there's less than an hour of unplanned time between school and bed. A few other signs worth noticing:

- They fall apart over small things right after school or right before bed.
- They say "I'm bored" almost never, because there's no time to be bored.
- They resist activities they used to love.
- Sunday night brings stomachaches or tears about the week ahead.
- Homework fights have gotten louder, and they're happening later.

None of these alone means you've done something wrong. Most families add activities one at a time, each for good reasons: swimming for safety, piano because Grandma played, soccer because their best friend joined. The schedule grows the way a closet fills up.

## What does Montessori say about free time and unstructured play?

Maria Montessori built her classrooms around long, uninterrupted work periods, often around three hours, where children chose their own activity and stayed with it as long as their interest held. She observed that children needed that stretch to reach real concentration, and that frequent interruptions left them scattered and restless.

Our daughter attends a Montessori school, and the part I've tried hardest to bring home is that respect for unhurried time. A child who gets to build, draw, sort a rock collection, or simply lie on the floor and hum is doing real work. That's where they practice settling their own mind.

The research backs this up. The American Academy of Pediatrics published a clinical report in 2018, "The Power of Play," that called free, child-directed play essential for building executive function, the brain skills behind self-control and planning. And a 2023 article in *The Journal of Pediatrics* by Peter Gray, David Lancy, and David Bjorklund argued that the long decline in children's independent, unsupervised activity is one likely contributor to rising anxiety in kids.

Positive parenting adds the relationship piece. Jane Nelsen's Positive Discipline approach suggests involving children in decisions that affect them, through family meetings and limited choices. Kids who help shape a plan are more likely to follow it, and they feel more capable for having been asked.

## How do I cut back on activities without my child feeling punished?

Make it a decision you make together, with clear limits you set as the parent. Your child gets a real voice, you keep the final say.

Here's a sequence that works well for 6- to 9-year-olds:

1. **Draw the week together.** On a big sheet of paper, sketch seven boxes. Let your child color in every scheduled thing, including school and after-care. Seeing it on paper often makes the point for you.
2. **Ask, don't announce.** "Which of these do you look forward to? Which ones feel like a lot?" Listen without arguing. You may be surprised which activity they'd happily drop.
3. **Set the frame.** "We're going to keep two weeknights completely free. You can pick which activities stay, as long as they fit in the other days." This is the firm part. The number of free nights isn't up for debate; the choice inside it is.
4. **Name what the free nights are for.** Building, playing outside, reading on the couch, helping cook. Avoid filling the free evening with a new class.
5. **Try it for a month, then check in.** Put a reminder on the calendar. Ask how sleep, mornings, and moods feel now.

If your child loves everything and won't choose, that's a sign of a full plate, too. You can make the call kindly: "I can see you love all of these. I'm choosing to pause swim until spring so your body gets more rest. We'll talk about it again in March."

## What does a good wind-down routine look like for this age?

A good wind-down is short, predictable, screen-free, and the same order every night. For most 6- to 9-year-olds, 30 to 45 minutes is plenty. Here's one shape you can adapt:

- **Screens off about an hour before lights out.** Bright, fast content keeps the brain alert, and sleep experts generally advise switching devices off well before bed.
- **Lights dim around the house.** Turn off the overhead lights and use lamps. It signals the evening is ending.
- **A prepared bedside spot.** In Montessori terms, set up the environment so your child can manage the routine alone: a basket with pajamas, a book, a small notepad and pencil, a water cup they can refill.
- **A "tomorrow" check.** Five minutes to lay out clothes and pack the backpack, so the morning isn't another source of worry at 10 p.m.
- **Connection time.** Ten minutes of reading or talking in the dark. This is often when kids tell you the real stuff about their day.

When our daughter was younger, I tried to cram a wind-down into ten rushed minutes after a late activity, and it never worked. What helped was accepting that the routine needed its own space on the calendar, the same as any class.

## When should I talk to a doctor about my child's sleep?

Check in with your pediatrician if your child regularly takes more than 30 minutes to fall asleep for several weeks even after you've lightened the schedule, snores loudly or seems to stop breathing during sleep, wakes often at night, or is very sleepy during the day. Also reach out if bedtime worries are intense, happen most nights, or come with stomachaches and school avoidance. These can point to sleep disorders or anxiety that deserve a professional look.

## Three Things I'd Do This Week

- Pick one weeknight and clear it completely. Tell your child on Monday so they can look forward to it.
- Move screens out of the bedroom and set a "devices go to sleep" time.
- Try the sticky-note worry trick for five nights and see if falling asleep gets faster.

My honest opinion: an empty Wednesday evening is one of the most underrated things you can give a 7-year-old. I also think most parents feel guilty for wanting fewer activities, when the real gift is a child who sleeps.

## FAQ

**How late is too late for a 7-year-old to fall asleep?**
It depends on wake time. Kids aged 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours of sleep. If your child wakes at 6:45 a.m., falling asleep after about 9:45 p.m. on school nights usually means they're not getting enough.

**How many after-school activities is too many for a 6- to 9-year-old?**
There's no fixed number, but if most weeknights are booked, dinner often happens in the car, and there's less than an hour of free time before bed, it's likely too much. Many families find one or two weeknights with no activity makes a noticeable difference.

**Should I let my child read in bed if they can't sleep?**
A paper book under a dim lamp is a reasonable choice. Avoid tablets and phones, since bright screens and engaging content tend to keep children alert. Set a gentle limit, such as one chapter, then lights out.

**What does Montessori say about over-scheduling?**
Montessori education values long, uninterrupted periods of self-chosen work, because that's when children build concentration. At home, that translates into protecting unstructured time where your child chooses what to do, rather than filling every evening with adult-led classes.

**Is it okay to let my child quit an activity mid-season to get more sleep?**
It can be. If sleep and mood are suffering, a pause is a reasonable health decision. Explain it as a choice about rest, involve your child in when they might restart, and if the team depends on them, help them finish a short commitment such as the next game.
