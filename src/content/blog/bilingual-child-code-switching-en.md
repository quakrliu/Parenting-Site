Last month at a birthday party in Richmond, BC — my friend Grace's youngest was turning four — my daughter was mid-sentence telling another kid about a toy she wanted to trade. What came out was: "Wǒ yào that one, the pink one, gěi wǒ." Half Mandarin, half English, one sentence, no pause between them. The parent standing next to me, someone I'd just met that afternoon, gave me a look that wasn't unkind, just concerned. "Is she getting the two languages mixed up?"

I've been asked some version of that question more times than I can count over the years, always from a caring place, always with the same assumption underneath it: that mixing two languages in one breath means a kid hasn't sorted them out yet. Here's what BloomPath's research on this actually shows, and it's the opposite of what most of us were taught to assume.

**TL;DR:** Mixing two languages within the same sentence — known as code-switching — is normal, developmentally expected behavior in bilingual children, not a sign of confusion or delay. Recent research shows bilingual kids often draw on one shared underlying grammar system rather than juggling two separate ones, and switching languages exercises the same brain circuitry used for attention and mental flexibility. Kids typically code-switch most when they're missing a word in one language, matching the language of the people around them, or simply reaching for whatever word comes fastest. The most useful response from parents isn't correcting the mix — it's modeling the language you want to reinforce and letting the conversation keep moving.

## Is It Normal for Bilingual Kids to Mix Languages in One Sentence?

Yes, and it's one of the most well-documented, most misunderstood behaviors in bilingual child development. A [2026 study covered by Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/cell-on-the-self/202607/why-bilingual-brains-switch-languages-without-asking) found that bilingual children often operate from one shared grammar system rather than two fully separate ones — which reframes what's actually happening when your kid slides from Mandarin into English and back again without missing a beat. It's not two systems colliding. It's one flexible system pulling whichever piece fits best in the moment.

I grew up assuming the opposite. My own parents used to gently correct me every time I mixed languages, convinced it meant I wasn't really fluent in either one. Turns out that assumption has been circulating in immigrant and bilingual households for generations, and it isn't backed by what researchers who actually study this have found.

## What Does the Research Say About Code-Switching and Executive Function?

Switching between languages recruits the same brain regions involved in executive function — the skills behind attention, mental flexibility, and shifting between tasks. Every time your child chooses which language fits a moment, or blends the two, that's a live rep of the same cognitive machinery used for things like ignoring distractions or switching between activities smoothly.

A [longitudinal study published on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7413223/) tracked French-English bilingual children at 36 and 61 months and looked specifically at how code-switching patterns related to executive function outcomes over time. The researchers found that kids tend to switch more often from their weaker language into their dominant one, and that a lot of switching happens to fill a lexical gap — meaning the word simply comes faster in the other language, not that the child is lost. That detail changed how I listen to my own daughter mid-sentence. She's not stuck. She's routing around a gap the fastest way she knows how.

## Why Does My Kid Code-Switch More at Some Times Than Others?

Kids code-switch more when they're missing a specific word, tired, excited, or talking to someone who also speaks both languages fluently. If your child mixes languages heavily with you but stays in one language with a monolingual grandparent or teacher, that's actually a strong sign they understand exactly who they're talking to and are adjusting on purpose — not evidence of confusion.

I've watched this shift happen in real time. At home, where both languages get used freely, the mixing is constant. At my friend Grace's house, where the other kids only speak English, my daughter stays almost entirely in English for the whole visit. She's not choosing that consciously in a way she could explain to you. But the switch happens, and it happens accurately, which is exactly the kind of social-linguistic tracking that takes real cognitive work.

## How Should I Respond When My Kid Mixes Languages?

Model the language you want to reinforce instead of correcting the mix mid-sentence. If your child says "I want nàge one," you can simply respond in the language you're aiming to build — "Oh, you want that one?" — without flagging the mix as a mistake. This keeps the conversation moving and gives your child a clean model of both languages without making the blend feel like something to be embarrassed about.

What doesn't help, in my experience and in what the research points toward, is stopping the conversation to insist on "say it all in English" or "say it all in Mandarin." That kind of correction doesn't speed up language separation — it just adds friction and self-consciousness to something that resolves on its own with exposure and time. Kids naturally shift toward using one language at a time more consistently as their vocabulary in each language deepens, particularly once they can reliably find words in both without needing to borrow.

## What We Actually Do at Home to Support Both Languages

In our house, Ethan mostly speaks English with our daughter, and Mandarin gets used heavily with extended family and in specific daily routines — mealtime, bedtime stories, a rotating stack of bilingual picture books. We never went strict "one parent, one language," partly because that's just not how our real daily life is organized, and partly because the research on code-switching gave me permission to stop worrying that flexibility would confuse her.

I'll admit I second-guessed this for longer than I should have. Even after reading a stack of bilingual parenting research, I still caught myself mentally counting how many sentences came out mixed during a car ride, like there was a score I needed her to hit. What actually shifted my thinking wasn't a single study — it was watching her, over months, switch cleanly into full English with an English-only cousin and back into full Mandarin with her grandmother an hour later, with zero effort and zero confusion on her part. The mixing at home was never the problem. It was doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does code-switching mean my child is behind in one language?**
No. Code-switching is most often a sign your child is drawing on whichever word comes fastest across two active language systems, not evidence that either language is underdeveloped. Kids who mix heavily at home often stay fully in one language when the situation calls for it.

**Should I correct my child every time they mix languages?**
It's not necessary and can add unhelpful self-consciousness. Modeling the language you want to reinforce, by responding naturally in that language, tends to work better than stopping the conversation to correct the blend.

**At what age does code-switching typically peak?**
It tends to be most frequent in the toddler and preschool years, when vocabulary in both languages is still growing unevenly. As vocabulary deepens in each language, most kids naturally use one language more consistently at a time, especially in single-language settings.

**Is code-switching different from being confused between languages?**
Yes. True confusion would look like inconsistent understanding of either language's rules or an inability to stay in one language even when the situation calls for it. Code-switching that adjusts based on who's listening is a sign of active, working language control, not confusion.

**Will code-switching go away as my child gets older?**
It usually decreases in everyday use as vocabulary fills in, but many bilingual and multilingual people, including bilingual adults, continue to code-switch comfortably for the rest of their lives — it's not something that needs to be "grown out of" to count as fluency.
