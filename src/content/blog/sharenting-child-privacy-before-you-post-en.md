Two weeks ago, at my daughter's school talent show, my father-in-law pulled out his phone during her act and started livestreaming straight to a family Facebook group with roughly 200 members — most of whom I have never met in person. Twelve minutes later I checked the group. The video already had nine comments. One of them tagged my daughter by her full name and named her school.

I didn't say anything at the time. It was his moment too, and making a scene in the school auditorium felt worse than the actual problem. But I sat there running the math in my head the way I run math on everything: 200 strangers, one livestream, her full name, her school, all searchable, all permanent, all decided by someone who wasn't her and wasn't me.

That's the moment I actually looked into sharenting — the practice of parents and grandparents posting kids' lives online — instead of just having a vague uneasy feeling about it. At BloomPath, we've spent two years building a parenting app specifically designed so families can keep their kids' milestones private by default, so this wasn't an abstract question for me. It was the exact problem sitting in my pocket that night.

## TL;DR

- Sharenting isn't rare — most parents who use social media post about their kids, but very few actually ask the kid first.
- The generation of kids who grew up "shared" online are now teenagers and adults, and some are pushing for laws to protect the next generation.
- AI image tools have added a new risk in the last two years that didn't exist when most sharenting advice was written.
- Grandparents and extended family are often the bigger privacy risk than parents themselves — and it's usually not malicious, just unexamined habit.
- You don't need to quit social media. You need a household rule that everyone who photographs your kid actually follows.

## What Is Sharenting, and Why Is Everyone Suddenly Talking About It in 2026?

Sharenting is the habit of parents posting details, photos, and milestones of their children's lives on social media, and researchers have been studying it seriously for close to a decade now. What's changed in 2026 isn't the behavior itself — it's who's old enough to react to it. The first kids who had their entire childhoods documented on Facebook by millennial parents are now teenagers and young adults, and some of them are not thrilled about it.

A 2024 CNN feature followed several of these now-grown "social media babies" who are lobbying state legislatures to protect kids whose images were shared or monetized without their input. It's not a fringe complaint anymore — it's become its own advocacy movement, with young adults describing the specific discomfort of having their awkward, tearful, or medical moments permanently searchable by classmates, employers, and strangers.

## How Many Parents Actually Post Photos of Their Kids Online?

More than 80% of parents who use social media have posted photos or videos of their kids, according to research reviewed by the American Psychological Association's Monitor in mid-2026. That part isn't surprising — most of us have done it.

What's more revealing is the consent gap. A study led by researchers at Indiana University's Luddy School, published in the Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, found that fewer than one in four parents ask their child for permission before posting about them, even though 81% use the child's real name in the post. A separate 2024 systematic review of sharenting research found that only about 4% of children reported direct negative outcomes like hurtful comments — but 12% had asked a parent to take something down. That number stuck with me. One in eight kids in that research had already, at some point, said "please remove that" about their own life.

## What Happens When Sharenting Kids Grow Up?

This is the part that changed how I think about it. A toddler photo in a bathtub isn't dangerous content by itself. What actually matters is consent and control accumulating over years — who made the call, and whether that call still belongs to the kid once she's old enough to have an opinion about it.

There's a meme among Gen Z kids right now: "finding your parents' Facebook." If you tell a classmate you found their parents' account, what you're really saying is you found years of embarrassing, unfiltered photos of them as a child, all searchable, all commentable, none of it something they chose. Some of those kids are now adults building "watch me grow up through my mom's Facebook" videos as content of their own — half joke, half genuine reckoning with how much of their childhood they didn't control.

Illinois became the first U.S. state, in 2024, to legally require parents who profit from monetized content featuring their kids to set aside a share of the earnings in a trust the child can access at 18. It's aimed at child influencers specifically, but the underlying idea — that kids have a stake in how their own image gets used — is the same idea driving the broader sharenting conversation. Legal scholar Stacey Steinberg, who has written extensively on children's digital privacy rights, has argued for years that parents should treat a child's consent the same way they'd treat any other person's: ask when you can, and think hard about it when you can't.

## Is There a New AI Risk I Should Know About?

Yes, and it's genuinely different from the risks sharenting research addressed even three or four years ago. Publicly posted photos of children can now be pulled by AI image tools and manipulated in ways that were technically impossible when most sharenting guidance was written. Several child safety researchers flagged this specifically in early 2026 as a reason to reconsider default-public sharing, not because every photo will be misused, but because the cost of a photo being public has gone up even if the photo itself hasn't changed.

I'm an engineer. I don't scare easily about hypothetical tech risks — most of them don't pan out. This one is different because the barrier to misuse dropped from "technically possible with effort" to "trivial," and that shift happened fast enough that most parents' mental model of "what could go wrong" is still a few years out of date.

## Why Are Grandparents Usually the Bigger Risk, Not You?

Because you've probably already tightened your own privacy settings and thought about this at least once. Extended family often hasn't — not out of carelessness, but because they grew up in a world where "sharing a photo" meant handing a print to one person, not broadcasting to 200.

The livestream at my daughter's talent show wasn't malicious. My father-in-law was proud. He wanted his friends to see her. The problem was structural: he had no framework for thinking about "200 people I've never met" as meaningfully different from "the neighbors I show photos to over coffee." Most oversharing from grandparents comes from that gap, not from disregard for the kid.

## How Do You Actually Handle This Without Starting a Family War?

The framing matters more than the rule itself — a household policy that applies to everyone, including you, lands very differently than correcting one person's post. Here's what's worked for us:

1. **Set one rule and repeat it, don't negotiate it per-photo.** Ours is: no full name + school + real-time location in the same post, ever, from anyone. It's simple enough that people actually remember it.
2. **Give people an easy alternative.** We started printing photos on an [Instax instant camera](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BWNZLQ69?tag=bloompath-20) at family gatherings specifically so grandparents have something physical to be proud of and hand around — without it going online at all.
3. **Ask before, not after.** If a grandparent wants to post something, the ask is "can you check with me first" — not "you're not allowed," which almost always turns into a fight.
4. **Talk to your kid about it as soon as they can understand the concept.** Even a five- or six-year-old can answer "is it okay if Grandma shares this one?" Teaching consent early does double duty — it protects them now and models something they'll need later.
5. **Back up what matters privately.** We keep our real photo archive on an [encrypted drive](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N6SNUZN?tag=bloompath-20) instead of relying on whatever's publicly posted somewhere as the "real" record.

## What Age Can Kids Actually Consent to Being Posted?

There's no legal age threshold in most places, but child privacy researchers generally suggest starting the conversation around age three — not because a three-year-old can meaningfully weigh long-term digital consequences, but because starting the habit early means it's normal by the time they actually can. By early elementary school, most kids can answer a direct question like "is it okay if I share this photo of you?" honestly. Whether you follow their answer is a separate conversation, but asking at all changes the dynamic.

## What Should You Do Instead of Posting Publicly?

This is where I'll be honest about my own bias: we built BloomPath partly because Mei and I wanted somewhere to actually keep our daughter's milestones — the drawings, the first words, the "she said the funniest thing today" moments — without deciding, in the moment, whether the entire internet should also have access to it. A private family space isn't a downgrade from social media. For most of what parents actually want to save, it's the better tool for the job.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is sharenting really harmful, or is this overblown?**
Most individual posts don't cause harm. The research concern is cumulative — years of unfiltered content, posted without consent, that a child has no control over and that becomes searchable by classmates, employers, and strangers as they grow up.

**What age can kids start giving real consent about being posted?**
Privacy researchers generally suggest starting to ask around age three, understanding that true informed consent develops later. The goal is building the habit of asking, not waiting for a "correct" age.

**How do I stop grandparents from oversharing without starting a fight?**
Set one simple household rule that applies to everyone rather than correcting individual photos, and offer an alternative like printed photos so they still get to share and feel proud.

**What's the new AI risk with children's photos?**
Publicly available photos can now be pulled and manipulated by AI image tools far more easily than even a few years ago, which changes the risk calculus even for ordinary, non-explicit photos.

**What should I do instead of posting my kid's milestones publicly?**
Keep a private family record — a shared album, an encrypted backup, or an app built for family use only — so you can still document everything without defaulting to public.
