It's 11:50 on a Thursday night. You walk past your 13-year-old's room and there's a blue line of light under the door. You knock, and the glow vanishes a half second too fast. "I'm sleeping," says a voice that was clearly not sleeping. Then comes Saturday: 8:00, 9:30, 11:00, the door still shut, the cereal bowl still in the sink, and a gnawing feeling that this was supposed to be the morning you all went to the market together.

If that rhythm sounds like your house, BloomPath wrote this for you. Nobody is lazy here, and nobody is being defiant on purpose. A teenager's sleep is being pulled by biology, a phone, and a school schedule at the same time, and the fix starts with a plan your teen helps build.

**TL;DR:** Puberty shifts the body clock later, so a 12-to-15-year-old often can't fall asleep at 9:30 even when they're tired. Most teens need about 8 to 10 hours. Keep the wake-up time roughly steady all week (within an hour or two), move the phone out of the bedroom overnight with your teen's agreement, and give them a say in the schedule. Rules your teen helped write get followed more often than rules handed down at 11:50 p.m.

## Why Does My Teen Stay Up Late and Sleep Until Noon?

Because the teenage body clock really does shift later during puberty. Sleep researcher Mary Carskadon spent decades documenting that adolescents release melatonin, the "it's getting dark, time to sleep" hormone, later in the evening than children or adults do. A teen lying in bed at 9:30 is often not being stubborn. Their brain hasn't gotten the signal yet.

Then the alarm goes off at 6:30 for school. A 13-year-old whose body clock says "I fall asleep around 11:30" and whose school says "be at your desk by 7:45" is running on 6 or 7 hours Monday through Friday. The weekend sleep-in is the body collecting a debt.

The sleep-medicine consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, which the American Academy of Pediatrics has endorsed, recommends 9 to 12 hours for ages 6 to 12 and 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18. Think of it like a phone battery: a teen on 6.5 hours a night is charging to 70 percent every day and wondering why the battery dies by Wednesday.

My own view, as a dad who still stays up too late himself: telling a teenager "just go to bed earlier" is like telling a person to feel hungry at a different time. You can shift it slowly, but you can't order it.

## Is It the Phone, or Is It the Body Clock?

Both, and they feed each other. The late body clock sets the stage, and the phone fills the extra hours. Bright light from a screen can delay melatonin even more, and the content matters as much as the light: group chats, short videos, and games are designed to keep you going. The AAP's family media guidance suggests screens off about an hour before bed and devices charging outside the bedroom overnight.

Here's the part that's easy to miss. For many teens, midnight is when the phone is most tempting because it's the only time nobody is asking them for anything. School ends, activities end, homework ends, and it's finally their own quiet time. If you only take the phone away, you've taken away the one slice of the day that feels like theirs. That's why a bare ban tends to produce a hidden phone and a frayed relationship.

So the useful question is "what job is the phone doing at midnight?" Usually it's one of three: winding down, staying in the loop with friends, or avoiding tomorrow. Each one calls for a different answer.

## How Many Hours of Sleep Does a 12-to-15-Year-Old Need?

Plan on 8 to 10 hours for most teens, with 12-year-olds often needing the upper end. Count back from the alarm: if school starts at 7:45 and your teen needs 8.5 hours plus 30 minutes to fall asleep, they should be in bed by about 9:30 and lights out by 10. If that sounds impossible for a night-owl 14-year-old, that's the honest reason the Academy of Pediatrics has recommended middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. Most families can't change the school bell, so you work with what you've got.

A realistic goal for a teen whose current bedtime is midnight is not 9:30 next week. Move bedtime 15 to 20 minutes earlier every few days, with a steady wake-up time, and the body clock tends to follow.

## What Should I Say When I Catch Them on the Phone at Midnight?

Skip the lecture at the door. A teen who's tired and caught can't take in a speech. Say one short thing, then save the real conversation for a calm moment.

At the door, try: "It's late. Phone on the kitchen charger now, we'll talk tomorrow." That's the firm part. Then the warm part: "I'm not mad. I want to figure out a plan that works for both of us."

The next day, in a calm moment (a car ride, a walk, a snack), try the three-step version:

1. **Ask first, and listen.** "What's going on at night? What do you do on your phone after 11?" You're gathering information.
2. **Share your worry in one sentence.** "I'm worried you're running on 6 hours and it's making mornings and mood harder."
3. **Ask for their ideas.** "What would help you actually get to sleep earlier? What would you want in exchange?"

Montessori's view of adolescence is that teens need real responsibility and real respect as emerging adults, and that they do best when they take part in making the rules that govern them. Positive Discipline, the framework by Jane Nelsen, comes at it from the same angle with family meetings and joint problem-solving. Both fit this situation neatly. A teen who proposed the 10:30 phone drop-off is much more likely to stick with it than one who got it as a verdict.

## What Does a Teen Sleep Plan Actually Look Like?

Write it down together. One page, one fridge, signed by everybody, parents included. A starting version might look like this:

- **Phones charge in the kitchen or hallway overnight** at a set time (say 10:00 on school nights, 11:00 on weekends). A cheap alarm clock replaces the phone as the alarm. Parents' phones go in the same spot, because "do as I say" lands badly if you're scrolling in bed yourself.
- **A wind-down window.** 30 to 45 minutes before the phone goes out, switch to something that isn't a feed: a shower, a book, music, a podcast, drawing, chatting with you.
- **A wake-up time that stays close all week.** Many sleep clinicians suggest keeping the weekend wake-up within about one to two hours of the weekday one, because big swings keep the body clock out of sync. A Saturday lie-in until 9:30 is fine. Noon on Saturday followed by midnight on Sunday sets up a rough Monday.
- **Morning light.** Opening the curtains or getting outside for 10 minutes after waking helps the clock shift earlier. In the dark weeks of November through January, this matters more.
- **A review date.** "We'll check in after two weeks and adjust." Plans that can change feel fair, and fair plans get followed.

I'd add one more thing from experience: put the consequence in the plan before you need it. "If the phone isn't on the charger at 10, it goes on the charger for the next night, no argument." Agreed in advance, it's a rule everyone saw coming. Delivered in the moment, it's a punishment.

## What If My Teen Won't Give Up the Phone at Night?

Start by checking what you're asking against what you're offering. If the phone is also their alarm, their music, and their only link to friends, "put it in the kitchen" is a big ask with nothing back. Offer something concrete: an alarm clock, a speaker for music, and a clear time when messages can resume in the morning.

If they push back, you can say: "I hear that this feels unfair, and the answer is still that phones sleep in the kitchen. Let's look at what part of the plan you'd change." Hold the line on where the phone sleeps, and stay flexible on the timing, the weekend rules, and the review date.

If they still sneak the phone, don't turn into a detective. Take it as a sign that the plan didn't solve their real problem, and go back to the "what is the phone doing for you at midnight?" conversation. [A child who hides things from you](/en/blog/child-hiding-mistakes-trust-repair-conversation-en) is usually protecting something, so repair first and rules second.

## When Is Teen Sleep Trouble More Than a Late Body Clock?

Talk to your pediatrician if you see any of these: your teen is exhausted despite 8 or more hours in bed, loud snoring or pauses in breathing, falling asleep in class regularly, a sharp drop in mood or interest in things they love, or sleep problems that last more than a few weeks even with a steady routine. Insomnia, sleep apnea, anxiety, and depression can all show up as "my teen won't sleep," and they deserve a professional look. If your teen talks about hopelessness or hurting themselves, contact a doctor or a crisis line the same day.

## How Do I Keep My Own Cool Through All This?

Know that you'll lose some of these evenings. Years in, I still get the tone wrong when I'm tired and a 13-year-old is rolling their eyes at me, and the best thing I've learned is that a calm "let's try again tomorrow" beats winning the argument tonight. If you got sharp, repair it in the morning: "I was short with you last night. I'd like to try that again." That sentence is worth more than any bedtime rule.

Also keep the ratio healthy. If every conversation about your teen's phone is about taking it away, the phone becomes the whole relationship. Make sure there's room for "I like that song" and "tell me about that game."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much sleep does a 13-year-old need?**
Most 13-year-olds need about 8 to 10 hours a night, according to the sleep-medicine consensus recommendation endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics. Many are getting 6 to 7 on school nights, so weekend sleep-ins are often catch-up sleep.

**Is it normal for a teenager to stay up late and sleep until noon?**
Yes. Puberty shifts the body clock later, so teens feel sleepy later at night and want to wake later. A steady schedule, morning light, and an earlier phone cutoff help, but expect some drift on weekends.

**Should I take my teen's phone at night?**
It helps most when the phone charges outside the bedroom overnight, and it works best when your teen helped set the rule. Offer an alarm clock as a replacement and keep your own phone out of the bedroom too.

**Is sleeping in on weekends bad for teens?**
A moderate sleep-in is fine and helps with sleep debt. Large swings, like waking at noon on Saturday and Sunday, make Monday harder because the body clock shifts later. Keeping wake-up within one to two hours of weekdays is a common recommendation.

**What time should a 14-year-old go to bed on school nights?**
Count back from the alarm: wake-up time minus 8.5 to 9 hours, plus time to fall asleep. For a 6:30 a.m. alarm, that usually means lights out near 9:30 to 10:00. Move toward it in small steps rather than all at once.

**When should I worry about my teen's sleep?**
Talk to a pediatrician if your teen is exhausted despite 8 or more hours in bed, snores loudly, falls asleep in class, shows a big mood change, or has sleep trouble lasting more than a few weeks.
