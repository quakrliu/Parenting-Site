Two Tuesdays ago, at our kitchen table in Taipei, my daughter looked up from her homework and asked me how to say 空氣清淨機 in English. I knew the word — I write about bilingual development for BloomPath for a living — but I couldn't find it fast enough, and by the time I got to "air purifier," she'd already grabbed her tablet to look it up herself.

I sat there for a second feeling like a fraud. All that reading, and I still froze on a word my kid could look up faster than I could recall it.

My friend Amara, who's raising her son trilingual in Toronto, texted me almost the same week: "I told him the wrong past tense in French again. I don't know if I'm setting him up or messing him up."

Neither of us is a native English speaker teaching our kids in a country where English isn't the dominant language, and neither of us has ever been sure we're doing it "right." So I went back to the actual research, not the Instagram version of it, to answer a question I've been quietly asking myself for years: does a parent's language need to be perfect for a kid to actually become bilingual?

The short answer is no. The longer answer is more useful.

## Does a Parent's English Need to Be Native-Level for a Child to Become Bilingual?

No — but proficiency still matters more than people want to admit.

Linguist Barbara Zurer Pearson, who has spent over three decades researching bilingualism, makes a distinction that changed how I think about this: the gap between native and non-native speakers isn't really about grammar mistakes or accent. It's about vocabulary richness. A parent who speaks a second language well, even imperfectly, still gives a child a far richer language environment than a parent who speaks it only at a survival level.

That's the actual threshold that matters — not perfection, but sufficiency. If you can hold a real conversation, read a picture book without stumbling every sentence, and talk through your kid's day in that language, you're providing enough. If you're translating word by word in your head before you speak, that's a different situation, and leaning more on books, shows, and native speakers in that language makes more sense than trying to carry the whole thing yourself.

## What Does the Research Say About Non-Native Parents Specifically?

It says social interaction beats media, every time.

Patricia Kuhl at the University of Washington ran a study exposing nine-month-old American babies to Mandarin — some through live, in-person interaction with a speaker, some through audio or video recordings only. The babies who got real human interaction picked up Mandarin phonetic patterns. The ones who only got recordings didn't, even when the recordings were identical in content.

I think about this every time I feel guilty for not buying another flashcard set. The thing that actually builds a kid's brain for a second language isn't a perfect recording of a native speaker. It's a real person, in the room, responding to them.

That's genuinely good news for a parent like me. My accent doesn't disqualify me. What disqualifies a language from sticking is silence — not using it, not being present in it, not making it part of daily life.

## What Is a Family Language Plan, and Do You Actually Need One?

It's a simple, written decision about which language goes where — and yes, having one on paper (even a messy one) is worth doing.

Ethan and I didn't write ours down until my daughter was already talking in both languages, which in hindsight was backwards. A plan doesn't need to be complicated. Ours ended up being three lines on a sticky note on the fridge for about a year:

- English at breakfast and in the car
- Mandarin everywhere else at home
- Whatever language a book is written in, we read it in that language — no translating on the fly

The "One Parent One Language" approach (OPOL) is the most commonly cited framework, where each parent consistently speaks one language to the child. We didn't do a clean OPOL split, because that's not how our household actually talks. What worked for us was picking specific contexts instead of specific people — a "context plan" rather than a "person plan." Either works. What doesn't work, based on what I've read and what friends with older bilingual kids have told me, is no plan at all, where the second language only shows up randomly and gets crowded out within a year or two.

## Will Mixing Languages (Code-Switching) Confuse My Child?

No. Code-switching is a sign of skill, not confusion.

This is the one I hear most often from worried parents, including past-me. When my daughter was younger, she'd start a sentence in English and finish it in Mandarin mid-thought, and I used to wince, thinking I was doing something wrong.

Linguist François Grosjean's work on bilingualism reframed this for me: a bilingual child isn't two monolingual children sharing one brain. They're one bilingual thinker who has access to two systems and picks whichever word arrives first, or whichever word captures the idea better, or whichever word the other person will understand. Adults who speak two languages do exactly the same thing — we just call it something else when we do it at dinner parties.

The pattern to actually watch for isn't mixing. It's a child who stops attempting a language altogether, or who understands it but refuses to produce it past a certain age with no growth over many months. That's worth a conversation with a speech-language pathologist. Switching mid-sentence isn't that.

## What If I Don't Know a Word in Front of My Kid?

Say so, and look it up together. That's not a failure — it's modeling exactly what you want them to do.

I used to feel like I had to perform fluency, like admitting "I don't know that word either" would somehow undercut the whole project. It's the opposite. When I told my daughter, at that kitchen table, "I know what that thing is, I just can't remember the English word right now — help me," she lit up. She wasn't disappointed in me. She got to be the expert for thirty seconds, and for a kid, that's basically Christmas.

A script that's worked for us:
- "I know this in Mandarin but I'm blanking on English — say it in whichever one comes first."
- "Let's look that one up together."
- "You just taught me a word. That's a good trade."

## Is There an Age When It's Too Late to Start?

Later than you'd expect, and earlier is not always better in the way people assume.

The research is clear that younger children pick up native-like pronunciation more easily, but comprehension, vocabulary, and functional bilingualism build at any age with consistent exposure. What actually predicts outcomes isn't the starting age — it's the amount and quality of ongoing exposure over years, not months. A four-year-old with two years of half-hearted exposure will likely end up less bilingual than a seven-year-old with two years of consistent, daily use.

If you're starting later than you planned, or restarting after a language dropped off during a busy season, the research doesn't punish you for the delay. It rewards consistency from wherever you start.

## FAQ

**Do I need to be a native speaker to raise a bilingual child?**
No. Research on non-native language input shows that a parent with strong (not perfect) proficiency provides a rich enough language environment for a child to become genuinely bilingual. The bigger risk factor is very limited proficiency, not an accent or occasional grammar mistakes.

**Will my grammar mistakes or accent confuse my child?**
No. Children exposed to imperfect but consistent input still acquire the language's grammar rules over time, often correcting patterns their parents got wrong, because they're also absorbing input from books, media, and other speakers.

**Is it bad if my child mixes two languages in one sentence?**
No — code-switching is a normal, even sophisticated, feature of bilingual development. It reflects a child fluidly accessing two language systems, not confusion between them. Watch for a child avoiding a language altogether over time, not for mixing.

**What's a family language plan, and do I need to write one down?**
It's a simple agreement about which language is used in which context (or by which person). Writing it down, even informally, tends to keep both languages present long-term. Households without any plan often see the weaker language fade within a year or two.

**What age is too late to start raising a bilingual child?**
There isn't a hard cutoff. Younger children acquire native-like pronunciation more easily, but vocabulary and functional bilingualism build at any age with consistent, ongoing exposure — consistency matters more than the starting age.
