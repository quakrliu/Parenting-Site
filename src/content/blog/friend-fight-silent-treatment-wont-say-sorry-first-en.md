Dinner is half over and your child is pushing rice around the plate. You ask about the weekend plan with the neighbor kid who's been in your kitchen every Saturday since first grade. Your child answers without looking up: "We're not friends anymore. She started it. I'm not saying sorry first, and neither is she."

Then a week goes by. At pickup the two of them walk out of the school gate ten steps apart, both pretending the other one is invisible. If that picture is familiar, BloomPath wrote this guide for you. It covers parents of 8 to 12 year olds and answers three questions: what to say tonight, what to avoid, and how to help your child take the first step toward making up without forcing a hollow apology.

## Quick Answer: What Should I Do When My Child Refuses to Apologize First?

Skip the "just say sorry" order and begin with the feeling underneath the pride: "Sounds like you're really hurt, and it's hard to go first when you feel she owes you one." Once your child calms down, split the problem in two: what happened, and what your child wants now. Practice one short, honest sentence together ("I didn't like how that went. Can we start over?") and let your child choose when and how to say it. You support the repair, and your child makes the move.

## Why Won't My 10-Year-Old Say Sorry First?

Because at this age, going first feels like losing. Between 8 and 12, friendships become the center of a child's social world, and so does the fear of looking weak in front of a friend. An apology that comes first can feel like admitting "it was all my fault," even when your child knows it wasn't.

A few things usually sit behind the stubbornness:

- **Hurt that hasn't been named.** "She started it" often translates to "she said something that stung and I don't know where to put that."
- **Fairness radar.** Children in this age band are intensely loyal to rules. If the other child never apologized for their part, an apology from your child feels like an injustice.
- **Not knowing the words.** Many kids have only ever said sorry because an adult stood over them. Without the grown-up pushing, they have no script for a real one.
- **A brain still flooded.** Daniel Siegel's "upstairs and downstairs brain" idea in *The Whole-Brain Child* explains why a child who is still angry can't reason about repair. The reasoning part simply isn't online yet.

Knowing which of these you're looking at changes your first sentence. A hurt child needs the feeling named. A child with fairness radar needs you to acknowledge that part of it really wasn't fair. A child without words needs a script.

## What Do I Say Tonight?

Pick a moment away from the fight: in the car, while you're chopping vegetables side by side, or at bedtime when the lights are low. Face-to-face at the dining table can feel like an interrogation for a tween. A short sequence that works for many families:

1. **Listen first, and keep your questions open.** "What happened at recess?" and then wait. Resist fixing anything for the first five minutes.
2. **Name the feeling you hear.** "That sounds embarrassing, especially with other kids watching." Skip the "but" for now.
3. **Say the unfair part out loud.** "It sounds like she didn't say sorry either. That makes it harder to go first." Your child needs to hear that their sense of fairness has been noticed.
4. **Ask what they want.** "Do you want to stay friends? Do you want some space? Both are okay." Many children haven't asked themselves this yet.
5. **Offer, don't order.** "If you'd like, we can figure out one sentence you could say. You can pick whether you ever use it."

I'll admit it took me longer than I'd like to learn that last step. Years in, I still catch myself wanting to solve it by tomorrow morning.

## What Should I Avoid Saying?

- **"Go say sorry right now."** A forced apology teaches the words without the meaning. Your child may say it with a flat voice, the friend hears it that way, and now there's a second layer of resentment.
- **"It's just a little thing, you'll be friends again by Friday."** It might be true. It also tells your child the feeling is silly.
- **"Who started it?"** Detective questions invite your child to build a legal case, and the argument moves from the playground into your kitchen.
- **"I'll call her mom."** Unless there's bullying or safety involved, stepping in takes away your child's chance to practice repair. (If you can't tell whether this is ordinary conflict or something more serious, [this guide on bullying versus peer conflict](/en/blog/bullying-vs-peer-conflict-signs-en) lays out the differences.)
- **"You're being stubborn."** Pride feels like protection to a child. Calling it stubbornness makes them dig in.

For a longer list of lines to retire, see [The Shame Trap: 10 Phrases That Damage Trust](/en/blog/shame-trap-blame-phrases-rewrite-en). The same reasoning shows up in [why forcing an apology doesn't work between siblings](/en/blog/kids-wont-stop-fighting-sibling-conflict-en).

## What Does an Apology That Actually Repairs Look Like?

Jane Nelsen, who developed Positive Discipline, describes three steps she calls the "Three R's of Recovery": recognize your part, reconcile by telling the other person, and resolve by working out together what to do next. Dr. Becky Kennedy's *Good Inside* makes a similar case for repair as a skill that can be learned, and a hard conversation after a rupture as a normal part of a close relationship.

For a 10-year-old, that can be a small, concrete recipe:

- **Say your part.** "I rolled my eyes when you picked the other team, and that was mean." One sentence, only about *your own* behavior.
- **Say what it's like for you.** "I felt left out, and I snapped."
- **Ask, don't demand.** "Can we start over?" A question leaves the friend room to say yes.

Notice that the recipe doesn't ask your child to take the blame for everything. A real apology covers your own piece, even when the other child has their own piece to own. Make this explicit at home: "You only have to apologize for your half. Her half belongs to her."

## What Does Montessori Say About Making the First Move?

Montessori classrooms teach "grace and courtesy" as concrete lessons, usually demonstrated with slow, silent role-play: how to interrupt politely, how to say "excuse me," how to come back after a disagreement. The teacher shows, the children practice, and nobody gets shamed in front of the group. The thinking is that children want to be kind, and they need to see the skill modeled before they can try it.

You can bring this home without any special materials:

- **Role-play with a stuffed animal or in the car.** You play the friend, your child tries the one-sentence apology, and you swap. Silly voices help a lot at 8 or 9. By 12, a short run-through while walking the dog does the job.
- **Let your child choose the format.** A handwritten note, a message with a funny sticker, a word at the lockers. Choice protects dignity.
- **Prepare the setting.** Montessori calls it the prepared environment. At home, that can mean having paper and pens in a spot where a note feels natural to write, so making up takes no effort.
- **Keep it private.** Group chats and the playground audience make it harder. A one-on-one moment lowers the stakes.

If your child watches you repair with your partner, a grandparent, or even with them after a hard evening, they gain the best model there is. "I was short with you this morning. I'm sorry. Can I try again?" costs ten seconds and teaches more than a lecture.

## What If the Silence Drags On?

Some fights fade on their own, and some friendships do change as kids grow. Both can be true. If a week or two passes and your child still looks unhappy, ask once, gently: "Do you miss her, or are you glad for the break?" The answer tells you whether to keep supporting repair or to help your child grieve and find new company. A friendship that ended isn't a failure of your child, and a [friend who found a new best friend](/en/blog/tween-best-friend-got-new-best-friend-en) is a different kind of hurt that deserves its own conversation.

It's also worth looking at the pattern. Does your child fight with the same friend in the same situations, like competitive games or group chats? If a [screen or phone is part of the conflict](/en/blog/kid-prefers-phone-over-seeing-friends-en), a family agreement about when messages happen can remove a recurring trigger.

## When Should I Be Concerned?

An argument followed by silence is ordinary at this age. Talk with your child's teacher or school counselor, and consider your pediatrician, if you see:

- Repeated exclusion, name-calling, or being left out of group chats on purpose.
- A child who doesn't want to go to school, who has stomachaches on school mornings, or who stops wanting to see any friends.
- Changes in sleep or appetite, or a long stretch of sadness or irritability.
- Any statement about hurting themselves or not wanting to be here. That one calls for action the same day. In the U.S. you can call or text 988 at any hour, and other countries have similar crisis lines.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal for a 10-year-old to refuse to apologize first?**
Yes. Between 8 and 12, friendship status matters a lot, and going first can feel like losing. Pride at this age usually covers hurt feelings.

**Should I make my child apologize?**
Requiring a sorry teaches the words without the meaning, and the other child can usually tell. Helping your child find an honest sentence about their own part works better.

**What if the other child was the one who was unkind?**
Your child can still own their small piece, such as raising their voice, and leave the rest with the friend. Tell your child that apologizing for their half doesn't mean agreeing with the other half. For repeated meanness, talk to the teacher.

**How long should I wait before stepping in?**
Give a typical argument a week or so. If your child seems more unhappy day by day, or you see signs of exclusion or bullying, talk with the school sooner.

**What does Montessori say about apologies?**
Montessori classrooms teach grace and courtesy through short, calm demonstrations and role-play. Children practice polite repair before they need it, so the words are already familiar when a real fight happens.

**Can I contact the other child's parent?**
It's reasonable when safety or bullying is involved. For ordinary friend fights, giving your child a chance to practice repair first usually serves them better.
