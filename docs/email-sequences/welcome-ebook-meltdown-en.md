```
Hi {{first_name}},

A story from 9 years ago.

I was in a department store with our daughter. She was **2½**. She'd fallen in love with a small yellow stuffed pillow in the toy aisle and carried it around for twenty minutes.

When I gently said "let's put it back, sweetheart," she dropped to the floor.

Then came the sound — the one I can only describe as "smoke alarm arguing with another smoke alarm."

Every adult within ten paces turned to look. I stood there, looking at her on the floor, looking at the pillow, and five thoughts arrived at once:

> "Pick her up? Walk away? Pretend she's not mine? Give in and buy the pillow?"

---

**I picked option four.**

I bought the pillow. The meltdown stopped instantly. Everyone exhaled. We left peacefully.

In that moment, it was the most efficient decision available.

But later — after reading Janet Lansbury's *No Bad Kids*, Dr. Becky's *Good Inside*, and Daniel Siegel's *The Whole-Brain Child* — I realized **it was the wrong call for the long run**.

Not because buying a pillow is bad.

But because my daughter's brain learned, in those 30 seconds:

> "When I cry loud enough, long enough, the thing I want appears."

That year, this kind of meltdown happened 3–5 times a week.

---

## If I could go back to that day

I'd do what's in the PDF you downloaded — the 90-second framework:

- **0–30s** → One breath. Steady myself first.
- **30–60s** → Kneel down. Name the feeling: "You really want this pillow. We're not buying it today. You're so sad."
- **60–90s** → Wait. No lecture. Stop talking. Just stay close.

She'd **still cry** — but it wouldn't be a power struggle anymore.

She'd be feeling the real emotion of *wanting something and not getting it*, with me **letting that feeling exist** beside her.

A few minutes later, she'd reach for my hand to stand up.

We'd put the pillow back. Walk out of that aisle. She'd wave goodbye to it.

---

Tomorrow I'll send a 60-second explanation of the brain science behind this — **why "calm down" makes meltdowns worse**.

If you haven't opened the PDF yet, [read it now](https://bloom-path.app/en/free/toddler-meltdown-playbook/read/) — tomorrow's email pairs with it.

— Mei

PS. The 90-second method isn't about **making kids stop crying immediately**. It's about not turning the meltdown into a parent-child fight. That difference is everything.
```

### Body

```
Hi {{first_name}},

Two days ago I said "calm down," "let me explain," "there's nothing to cry about" — none of these work.

Today, the 60-second version of why.

---

## Two brain regions

Your child's emotional life runs on these two:

**Amygdala**
- Job: emotion, fight-or-flight
- When online: **fully developed by age 1**
- Think: gas pedal

**Prefrontal Cortex**
- Job: rational thought, impulse control, emotion regulation
- When online: **fully developed around age 25**
- Think: brake

---

## So...

When your 2-year-old melts down because the cracker broke:

- Amygdala is 100% online
- Prefrontal cortex is maybe 10–20% online

In other words: **the gas pedal is floored, the brakes literally aren't installed yet.**

Overheating is inevitable. It's not a bug. It's a developing nervous system doing exactly what it does.

---

## What's happening when you "explain"?

When they're in full meltdown, **the prefrontal cortex is essentially offline**.

You're telling them "it's cold outside so you need pants," "you can't do that to your sister," "next time don't do this" —

These rational messages **have nowhere to land**.

You're typing commands into a computer that just crashed.

---

## So what works?

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child has decades of research on something called **co-regulation**:

> **A calm adult next to the child gradually brings the child's nervous system back to baseline. It usually takes 5–15 minutes.**

That's the science behind the "60–90s wait" stage in the PDF.

You're not waiting for them to "understand." You're loaning them your calm so their nervous system can downshift.

---

## Three books if you want to go deeper

- ***The Whole-Brain Child*** (Daniel Siegel) — this concept across the whole book
- ***Good Inside*** (Dr. Becky Kennedy) — translates the brain science into parent language
- ***No Bad Kids*** (Janet Lansbury) — especially good for ages 1–3

You don't need to read all of them. **Use the PDF first.** Pick up these books later if you want depth.

---

I'll be quiet for 2 days, then on Day 5 I'll share something we're working on next.

— Mei

PS. If you try the PDF method on one meltdown this week — **whatever the result** — hit reply and tell me. I read every one.
```
